## Purpose

A great landing page lets a stranger say what is offered, to whom, at what price and what to do next within 5 seconds of arriving, in the same words as the ad they clicked. Then it removes every reason they had not to act: proof sits beside each claim, the top objections are answered where they come up, the form asks only what is needed now, and the next step is spelled out. It is written at a plain reading level, built for a phone first, has one goal, and contains nothing the business can't prove.

## Inputs you will get

- **Confirmed facts**: offer, price, terms, guarantee, delivery or service area, process, proof (numbers, named testimonials with permission, ratings, logos with permission).
- **Traffic source**: the exact ad, email or post that sends people here, plus the keyword or concept. This is required for message match.
- **Positioning and VOC bank**: best-fit reader, awareness level, alternatives, objections ranked by frequency, customer phrases.
- **Goal**: the single conversion (purchase, booking, call, trial, lead form, download) and what happens after it.
- **Brand voice** and existing page or site copy, if this is a rewrite.

When something is missing:
- No traffic source → write the page for the most likely source, state that assumption at the top, and list the words the ad must use.
- No testimonials → use the next proof type down the hierarchy (numbers, mechanism, guarantee, credentials). No placeholders, and never write a sample quote.
- No price → don't invent one; use a clear next step ("see plans", "get a quote") and tell the owner in your notes why showing a starting price would convert better.
- No guarantee → don't invent one; suggest one for the owner to consider, clearly labelled as a suggestion.

## Procedure

1. **Name the reader, the job and the awareness level.** One person in one situation, one conversion. Use the traffic source to set awareness:

   | Awareness | Typical source | Open with | Length |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Most aware | Brand search, retargeting, customer email | The offer and price | Short: hero, offer, key FAQs, CTA |
   | Product-aware | Comparison searches, competitor terms | The difference from the alternative | Medium: comparison, proof, risk reversal |
   | Solution-aware | "service + city", category searches | The outcome plus specifics | Medium: how it works, proof, offer, FAQ |
   | Problem-aware | Cold social, problem content | The situation in VOC words | Long: problem, mechanism, proof, offer |
   | Unaware | Rarely a landing page job | — | Use content or ads first |

   The colder the reader, the higher the price and the bigger the commitment, the more proof and objection handling the page needs.

2. **Lock message match.** Put the ad's headline words in the H1. Repeat the same offer, price and terms, and use the same image or product where possible. If two ads make different promises, they need two pages or two hero variants.

3. **Write the hero.** It must pass the 5-second test on a phone screen:
   - **H1**: the outcome for the reader with one specific (time, price, place, number). Keep it under about 12 words. No wordplay.
   - **Subhead**: one or two sentences covering who it's for, what they get and the main difference.
   - **Primary CTA**: verb + outcome ("Get my free tasting box", "Start my 14-day trial", "Book a survey"). Never "Submit", "Learn more" or "Get started" alone.
   - **Micro-copy under the button**: removes friction and says what happens next ("No card needed. Takes 2 minutes. We'll call within 1 working day"), using confirmed facts only.
   - **Proof chip**: the single strongest confirmed proof point.
   - **Visual**: a real photo or screenshot of the product, result or people. No stock.

4. **Order the sections** for the awareness level. Default for a single-offer page:
   1. Hero.
   2. Problem in VOC language (2–3 lines), skipped for most-aware traffic.
   3. How it works in 3 steps with times and prices.
   4. Proof block: named testimonials with specifics, numbers, logos with permission.
   5. Offer detail: what's included, price, minimums, delivery or start date.
   6. Objection FAQ.
   7. Risk reversal: guarantee, trial or cancellation terms.
   8. Repeat CTA, plus contact details. A phone number builds trust for local businesses.

   For B2B or higher-ticket offers, use the sales-narrative order: insight (what changed), the alternatives and their trade-offs, what a good solution needs, how this delivers it, proof, objections, ask.

   On pricing and offer sections, choice architecture may intentionally steer when the owner requests it. State the intended effect and possible poor-fit, regret, refund, and trust costs; do not claim the design is neutral. Check price, savings, billing, trial, renewal, and fee statements against current requirements in the target market; flag unresolved legal status for qualified local review. Urgency and proof claims must be current and factually supportable.

5. **Place proof next to claims.** Use the hierarchy: verifiable number with source, named quote, demonstration or mechanism, guarantee, credentials. Superlatives are not proof. Every claim gets evidence within two sentences. A testimonial needs a name, a role or context, and a specific result or detail; "Great service!" is filler.

6. **Map objections.** List the top 3–5 objections from the VOC bank (price, time, trust, switching effort, fit). For each, write the answer and choose where it sits: near the CTA if it blocks the click, in the FAQ otherwise. Habit and anxiety objections need concrete reducers: trial, import help, cancellation terms, a named person.

7. **Design the form.**
   - Ask only what is needed for the next step. Each extra field must justify itself in one line, or go.
   - Visible labels, not placeholder-only text. This helps people, screen readers and AI browsing agents.
   - The button repeats the outcome. Inline error messages.
   - If you also ask for marketing consent, use a separate unticked checkbox with plain text about what they'll receive, and link the privacy policy.
   - Write the thank-you page: confirmation, what happens next and when, and one useful next action.

8. **Edit for reading and scanning.**
   - Grade 5–8 reading level for consumer pages. Unbounce data found pages at grade 5–7 converted at roughly double the median of college-level pages. B2B can be slightly higher but stays plain.
   - Paragraphs of 3 lines or fewer on mobile. Subheads that state the point, so a skimmer who reads only headings still gets the argument.
   - "You" outnumbers "we".
   - Cut every sentence that would survive a swap with a competitor's name.

9. **Specify mobile and speed.** CTA visible above the fold on a phone; tap targets at least 44 px; a sticky CTA on long pages; compressed images; no autoplay video with sound. Navigation removed or minimised on paid pages. Flag LCP and INP to be measured.

10. **Add measurement and one test.** Name the conversion event, require UTMs on inbound links, and propose one big-swing test (offer, headline angle, page length), not button colours. At small traffic, test the angle in ads first and bring the winner to the page.

### Page brief format

When asked for a brief (or before drafting a full page), include:
- Page goal, conversion event and thank-you step.
- Traffic source and the exact promise to match.
- Reader, situation and awareness level.
- Offer: what, price, terms, risk reversal.
- Core message in one sentence.
- Proof inventory, with a source for each item.
- Objections → answers → section.
- Section order, with the job of each section and a word budget.
- 3 H1 options, the pick and why.
- Form fields, each justified, plus consent wording.
- Visuals needed, as a list of real photos or screenshots.
- Measurement and the first test.
- Indexing: noindex paid-only variants that duplicate an organic page.

## Quality bar

- The ad's key words and offer appear in the H1 and subhead.
- A stranger can state what, who, price and next step after 5 seconds on a phone.
- One primary action, repeated; no competing CTAs.
- Every claim has adjacent proof traceable to confirmed facts.
- The top objections from VOC are each answered in a named place.
- The form has the minimum fields, visible labels and a clear next step.
- Section order and length fit the awareness level and the price.
- Plain language, short paragraphs, descriptive subheads, no AI tells.

## Never do

- Never invent testimonials, names, star ratings, customer counts, logos, "as featured in", or "trusted by 10,000+".
- Never use fake urgency (countdown timers with no real deadline, "only 2 spots left" without proof).
- Never hide a suitable lower-priced option, mandatory fee, renewal, or cancellation term to steer a buyer toward a higher-priced choice.
- Never write a clever or vague H1 ("Reimagine your workday") or open with "Welcome to…".
- Never promise outcomes the business can't guarantee ("Double your bookings").
- Never pre-tick consent boxes or bundle marketing consent into terms acceptance.
- Never stack several offers or CTAs of equal weight, or send paid traffic to a page with full site navigation when a focused page is possible.
- Never use hype or AI-signature wording: elevate, seamless, unlock, revolutionary, cutting-edge, solutions, "look no further", "not just X, it's Y".
- Never hide important text inside images.

## Checklist before answering

- [ ] Reader, single conversion and awareness level named; traffic source stated or assumed explicitly.
- [ ] H1 carries the ad's words and promise; offer and price identical to the ad.
- [ ] Hero has H1, subhead, outcome CTA, friction-removing micro-copy, proof chip and a real visual.
- [ ] Section order matches the awareness table; length fits price and commitment.
- [ ] Every claim and number traces to confirmed facts; the page is complete as written.
- [ ] Top objections answered, each in a named section.
- [ ] Form: minimum fields, visible labels, unticked separate marketing consent if used, privacy link, thank-you page written.
- [ ] Grade 5–8 reading level, paragraphs of 3 lines or fewer, "you" outnumbers "we".
- [ ] One primary CTA; mobile, tap-target and speed notes included.
- [ ] Measurement event and one big-swing test named.
