name: competitor-teardown
description: Evidence-based competitive analysis for a small business from public web information. Use in research phases and when a positioning, growth-strategy, ad or sales job needs to know who the business is really up against. Builds a three-ring competitive set (direct, substitutes, aspirational), tears down messaging, pricing and offers verbatim, records channel footprint from ad libraries, search results and directories, mines competitor reviews with counts, maps the field on buyer-relevant axes, finds gaps with evidence and a why-empty check, and writes one battlecard per direct competitor.
---

## Purpose

You show the owner the field as a buyer sees it, with receipts. A great teardown tells them who they actually lose to (often not a company: a spreadsheet, a cheaper local guy, doing nothing), what each rival claims in its own words, what it charges, where it shows up, what its customers complain about and how often, and which gaps are real enough to build a position or offer on. Every observation is dated and sourced. "Competitor X has weak SEO" is not an observation; "Competitor X has no page for 'emergency boiler repair Leeds' and does not appear in the top 10 for it on 17 Oct 2026" is.

## Inputs you will get

- **Business brief:** business type, offer, price points, geography, website, the owner's named competitors (if any), lost deals and to whom (if known).
- **VoC research** (from voc-research), when available: alternatives customers mention, competitor-review themes with counts.
- **Research budget:** searches and fetches. Plan to it; competitor review mining and pricing pages are worth more than social follower counts.
- **In the drafting phase:** research notes with URLs and capture dates, plus confirmed facts.

When something is missing:
- No named competitors: build the set from search results and VoC, and say how.
- Pricing hidden ("contact us", "get a quote"): record "not published". Never estimate a competitor's price unless a public source states it, and then cite it.
- A source you could not access (ad library rendered by JavaScript, login wall): mark "not checked" and give the owner a one-line manual check (what URL, what to look for, about 5 minutes).
- Anything you cannot verify: `[NEEDS FACT: …]`.

## Procedure

1. **Build the competitive set in three rings.**
   - **Direct (3–5):** the ones the best-fit buyer actually compares. Find them through high-intent searches (`[service] [city]`, `best [category] for [segment]`, `[business] alternatives`, `[category] vs`), "switched from" mentions in reviews, and category directories (G2 category pages, Clutch, Checkatrade, app-store "similar apps", Amazon search for the product type). For local services: the top map-pack results for the 3 highest-intent queries in the service area.
   - **Substitutes (2–3), at least one non-product:** DIY, spreadsheet, hiring someone, an incumbent tool used "well enough", a marketplace (Upwork, Etsy, Amazon), or doing nothing. Source them from VoC.
   - **Aspirational (1–2):** players who set buyer expectations (the national brand, the well-funded app) even if the business can't match them.
   Exclude giants the buyer never mentions. State why each one is in the set.

2. **Messaging teardown (each direct competitor).** Capture verbatim with the URL and date: hero headline, subhead, category label used, who it says it's for, top 3 claims, proof types shown (logos, numbers, reviews, awards), primary CTA, risk reducers (trial, guarantee, free quote). Then classify:
   - Positioning style: head-to-head, niche (big fish, small pond), or category creation.
   - Main value theme: price, speed, quality, expertise, convenience or specialisation.
   - 10-second clarity: could a stranger say what it is, who it's for and why it's better? Yes / partly / no, with the reason.

3. **Pricing and offer teardown.** Record: pricing model (per seat, usage, flat, per project, retainer, per job, per head), entry price, the anchor tier (the one designed to look like the obvious choice), free trial / freemium / free quote, contract length and cancellation terms, guarantees, discounts, bundles, lead magnet. Normalise to one comparable unit (per seat per month, per typical job, per order) and show the maths. Hidden pricing is itself a signal; note it.

4. **Channel footprint (observable evidence only).**
   - **Search:** which high-intent queries they rank for in the results you saw, whether they have comparison, alternatives, pricing or location pages, and whether they are named in AI answers if you checked (state the prompt and date).
   - **Paid:** Meta Ad Library, Google Ads Transparency Center, LinkedIn Ad Library (B2B). Record active ads, the angles and offers used, and start dates. Ads running for months are *likely* profitable; say "likely", never "proven". Ad libraries generally do not show commercial spend or results; never state or estimate a competitor's ad spend.
   - **Social:** platforms used, posts in the last 30 days, typical engagement, whether a founder or employee is the visible face.
   - **Marketplaces and directories:** review count, average rating and review velocity (reviews in the last 90 days) on G2, Capterra, Clutch, Google, Checkatrade, Amazon, app stores.
   - **Other:** partnerships and integrations, communities, newsletter, podcast, events.

5. **Mine competitor reviews** (use the voc-research method; reuse its bank if present). For each direct competitor list: what customers love (do not attack this; respect it or match it), what they complain about with counts (n of N reviews read), and the words they use. Read critical reviews (1–3 stars) first; they name the trade-offs.

6. **Map the field on two axes buyers care about.** Pick axes from VoC themes, not from your imagination: e.g. "done-for-you vs. DIY", "generalist vs. specialist", "fixed price vs. hourly", "same-day vs. scheduled". Place each competitor and substitute with a one-line evidence note. Identify the empty or weakly held position.

7. **Gap analysis.** List gaps of four kinds, each with evidence:
   - **Message gap:** a value customers mention that no competitor leads with.
   - **Segment gap:** a buyer group that is underserved or ignored.
   - **Offer gap:** nobody offers a guarantee, fixed price, fast start, monthly option, small first step.
   - **Channel gap:** competitors absent where buyers are (no one answers in the subreddit, no comparison pages, no YouTube how-tos).
   For every gap run the **why-empty check**: is it empty because nobody noticed, or because it doesn't work (unprofitable, regulated, buyers don't value it)? State a risk and the cheapest test.

8. **Threat note.** Name the single competitor move in the next 6 months that would hurt most (price cut, copying the gap, a new entrant, platform change) and the early warning sign to watch.

9. **Battlecards: one per direct competitor**, one screen each:
   - Who they win with (segment, situation).
   - Their core claim (verbatim) and strongest proof.
   - Price (normalised, with source and date) or "not published".
   - Weak spots from reviews, with counts.
   - Where they are genuinely better (be honest; this tells the owner when to walk away or refer).
   - Our position against them in one sentence.
   - A **landmine question**: a fair, verifiable question a buyer can ask that exposes the weak spot ("Is the call-out fee included in the quote?"). It must be true of the competitor based on public evidence, not insinuation.

10. **Summarise for the owner.** Top 3 gaps ranked by evidence strength and fit with what the business can actually deliver, table-stakes features or proof the business is missing (parity it must reach), and what not to copy.

## Quality bar

- **Verbatim and dated.** Claims, headlines and prices are quoted exactly, with URL and capture date.
- **Three rings.** At least one non-product substitute is present, sourced from customer evidence.
- **Comparable pricing.** Prices are normalised to one unit with the maths shown, or explicitly "not published".
- **Counted complaints.** Every weak spot carries n of N reviews read and where.
- **Axes from buyers.** The field map uses dimensions customers talk about, each placement justified.
- **Gaps survive scrutiny.** Each gap has evidence, a why-empty check, a risk and a test.
- **Fair battlecards.** They concede where a rival is better and use landmine questions that are true and checkable.
- **Gaps checked against capability.** Recommendations fit what this business can actually deliver.

## Never do

- Write hollow judgements ("weak SEO", "outdated website", "poor branding") without the observation behind them.
- Estimate a competitor's revenue, ad spend, traffic or customer count unless a public source states it; then cite it.
- Treat a long-running ad as proof of profitability.
- Make false, unverifiable or disparaging claims about a competitor, or suggest the business does so in comparative copy. Comparative claims must be accurate and verifiable.
- Recommend copying a competitor's copy, design or brand assets.
- Scrape behind logins, use fake accounts, or request quotes under false pretences.
- List only named companies and miss the status-quo alternative.
- Present a gap as an opportunity without asking why it is empty.
- Mix observation dates silently; pricing and ads change, so every row carries its date.

## Checklist before answering

- [ ] 3–5 direct competitors, 2–3 substitutes (one non-product), 1–2 aspirational; inclusion reasons stated.
- [ ] Messaging teardown is verbatim with URL and date for each direct competitor.
- [ ] Pricing normalised to one unit or marked "not published"; no estimated prices.
- [ ] Channel footprint uses observed evidence only; unchecked sources listed with a manual check for the owner.
- [ ] Competitor complaints carry counts (n of N reviews read).
- [ ] Field map axes come from VoC themes, with evidence per placement.
- [ ] Every gap has evidence, a why-empty check, a risk and a cheapest test.
- [ ] One battlecard per direct competitor, including where they are better and a true, checkable landmine question.
- [ ] Threat note names one move and its early warning sign.
- [ ] No unsourced numbers; no disparaging or unverifiable comparative claims.
