## Judge reasons (Opus, v1 shown as A)
### plausible / strategy: v2
- B names the real constraint. $500 a month against more than 21,000 subscribers can add only a handful of customers, so trial activation and high-intent switcher pages come first and ads are a capped test with a kill line. A defaults to ads as priority 1 without asking whether $500 can matter.
- B's measurement fits the brand: no Google tag, UTMs read in Plausible, billing cohorts and a 'how did you hear about us' field. A links conversion tracking to the ad platform, which sits awkwardly with a no-cookies, independent brand. A top specialist would flag that.
- B has an affordable-cost formula, a kill line, sample-size maths with labelled placeholders, a decision point at week 6, a fallback and a reasoned not-doing list. That is genuinely prioritised and actionable for this business.
- B flags Google trademark policy, owner approval for running ads at all, and email consent. A misses these.
- A is accurate, clean and easy for a non-expert, but generic. Much of it restates the insights, and its experiments copy the insights' stopping rules without checking whether the traffic exists.
- B is long and dense, but Plausible is a sophisticated analytics team, so the depth is usable rather than padding.
### plausible / linkedin: v2
- B's posts are written natively for LinkedIn. Each opens with a strong hook ('Your site has a cookie banner. Do you know which tool on it is the reason?'). Each gives useful value first: a Network-tab check for script weight, an audit of which scripts need a banner, and three questions to ask any vendor. Each ends with a question that invites comments.
- A reads like three short ad blurbs: a feature line followed by 'learn more at plausible.io'. Posts like that get little reach or engagement on LinkedIn, and they sound generic rather than distinctive.
- B puts the call to action in the first comment, using the live demo and the 30-day no-card trial. That fits the 90-day paying-customer goal better than A's vague 'learn more' links.
- B handles the privacy claim responsibly. It says plainly that the post describes what the product does, is not legal advice, and that other tools may still need a banner. That matches the brand's transparent voice and limits legal risk.
- B's visual briefs are disciplined. They rule out invented file sizes, performance scores and logos, so the owner can use them directly.
- Both rely mainly on confirmed facts, and neither invents numbers or customers.
### formkit / strategy: v2
- B makes the key strategic call A misses. At $6 a seat with a generous free plan, the bottleneck is free-to-paid conversion, not traffic. B makes the product-led upgrade path primary and runs a holdout test on prompts shown at the moment a free user tries a Pro feature. That is the highest-leverage move for this business.
- B treats the $600 ad budget honestly. Search runs as a capped headline test with pre-set rules for scaling, extending or stopping, and unspent budget is not forced into other channels. A puts the full $600 a month into search ads without checking whether a $6 seat can ever pay that back.
- B is far more specific to Formkit. It names the Pro trigger features (payments, custom domain, badge, AI cap) and includes word use/avoid lists tied to brand voice. It also explains why HR, teachers and Meta/LinkedIn are excluded, and gives owners, hours and weeks for each task.
- A is clean, honest, on-voice and easy for a non-expert to follow. It never overclaims and gives sensible first actions. But it is a generic SaaS playbook with weaker prioritisation.
- B is too heavy for a small-business owner. It has jargon (modified ICE scores, CAC formulas), many unfilled placeholders, an assumed developer budget and a messy, truncated phases section. That narrows its lead.
### formkit / linkedin: v2
- B is far more specific and strategic. It builds each post around a real buying moment: what comes off each payment, building a form inside an assistant you already use, and outgrowing Google Forms. These match the strongest opportunities in the insights. A's posts read like short feature announcements.
- B is native to LinkedIn. It opens with strong hooks, gives readers useful takeaways (check the per-payment line on a pricing page, test the form yourself, find the question where people drop off) and ends with sharp questions. A's posts are thin and generic, with weak 'would you use this?' calls to action.
- B is unusually honest. It notes that Stripe still charges its own fees and labels the example prompt as illustrative. It also flags the free AI cap and marks unverified items as NEEDS FACT instead of guessing.
- A is cleaner on strict sourcing and ready to post as written, but it is too bland to win paying customers on a $600/month plan.
- B has some extra work for the owner: several first comments contain NEEDS FACT placeholders, and one hashtag is missing its '#'. These are minor and easy to fix.
