# Book 01 — Business Fundamentals, Economics & Competitive Strategy

Edition: October 2026. Research cutoff: October 19, 2026.

## 1. Start with the business mechanism

A growth strategy explains how a business can create more valuable customer relationships while remaining able to deliver its promises. It connects customer demand, an attractive offer, distribution, operations, and cash. A marketing activity belongs in that strategy only when the team can describe which connection it strengthens. Publishing more material may increase attention without improving demand. Buying more traffic may expose a broken onboarding process. Selling more annual subscriptions may improve this month's cash while creating a service obligation that lasts a year.

The first discipline is therefore to write the business mechanism in ordinary language. Who experiences a costly problem? What do they do today? Who can authorize a purchase? What changes after buying? How does the business receive money? What must it spend to deliver the result? What brings the customer back? These questions work for software, professional services, commerce, local services, and businesses selling through distributors. Their answers differ, so the same marketing recommendation cannot be transferred indiscriminately between them.

Consider a hypothetical bookkeeping service. Its problem might be late financial reporting for small employers. The buyer may be the owner, while an office manager supplies documents. Revenue arrives monthly, but onboarding requires concentrated labor. More leads are useful only if qualified prospects understand the reporting promise and the team has capacity to onboard them. A plan that maximizes booked calls while ignoring onboarding hours can increase apparent demand and reduce service quality simultaneously.

In ClimbOS, capture this mechanism as a dated business snapshot before selecting channels. Represent uncertainty explicitly: an unknown buying role is a research task, not permission to invent one. Store owner statements separately from integration measurements. The snapshot should make it possible to reconstruct why a recommendation was sensible at the time, even after prices, product capabilities, or market conditions change.

This book develops an original decision method for that work. The calculations and examples are educational scenarios, not industry benchmarks or reported results. Its strategic foundations include Porter's emphasis on distinctive activities, fit, and trade-offs, rather than merely performing familiar activities more efficiently. [Harvard Institute for Strategy and Competitiveness](https://www.isc.hbs.edu/strategy/creating-a-successful-strategy/Pages/default.aspx).

## 2. Separate goals, diagnosis, strategy, and tactics

A goal names an outcome. A diagnosis explains the constraint preventing it. A strategy chooses a coherent way to address that constraint. Tactics are the individual actions that implement the choice. Confusing these levels produces plans that sound active but cannot guide difficult decisions. “Grow revenue” is a goal; “post daily” is a tactic; neither explains why a customer should buy or why that action deserves scarce resources.

Write a diagnosis as a falsifiable causal statement: “Qualified buyers abandon evaluation because implementation effort appears greater than the expected benefit.” Then list the evidence and competing explanations. Perhaps prospects are simply outside the target segment. Perhaps the product lacks a critical capability. Perhaps prices are unclear. The diagnosis remains provisional until evidence distinguishes these possibilities. A useful strategy might reduce implementation uncertainty through a narrow onboarding offer, demonstrable setup steps, and explicit qualification.

Use a four-part strategy memo. First, state the customer and situation being prioritized. Second, identify the obstacle with its evidence. Third, describe the chosen approach and the activities it requires. Fourth, state what will be deferred. The last part matters because a strategy that accepts every opportunity does not resolve resource conflicts. An inexpensive self-service offer and a deeply customized enterprise service can both be attractive businesses, but combining them without separate economics can make each perform poorly.

A practical decision tree begins with evidence of a real problem. If evidence is weak, research comes before scaling acquisition. If demand exists but customers do not activate, improve the path to value. If activation works but retention is weak, investigate recurring usefulness and service quality. If retained customers are profitable and delivery capacity exists, test more distribution. If the business cannot finance the delay between spending and collection, fix financing or payment terms before increasing acquisition commitments.

ClimbOS should render these branches as a recommendation with supporting observations, alternative explanations, and a next discriminating test. Avoid a single opaque “growth score.” Two businesses can receive the same score while needing opposite actions. A structured diagnosis preserves the causal reasoning the owner needs to challenge. Its acceptance criterion is that another reviewer can explain what finding would make the recommendation change.

## 3. Model the business without mistaking the map for evidence

The Business Model Canvas offers a compact way to relate customers, value, delivery, resources, revenue, and costs. Strategyzer presents it as a model of how a business creates, delivers, and captures value, with nine connected blocks. The canvas is a useful organizing reference; a completed canvas does not establish that the underlying assumptions are true. [Official Business Model Canvas](https://www.strategyzer.com/library/the-business-model-canvas).

For practical use, attach an evidence state to each important connection. A customer segment might be supported by invoices, interviews, or only the founder's belief. A distribution channel might exist technically but never have produced a retained customer. A partnership might be signed but inactive. This makes the model operational: the most consequential weak connection becomes a candidate for validation. The objective is not to fill every box with confident prose. It is to identify which unsupported belief could make the business fail.

Take a hypothetical Mac application sold with a perpetual license. The customer receives local utility and may expect compatibility updates. Revenue arrives once, while support and maintenance continue. A subscription model for the same application changes the promise: customers may now judge whether continuing value justifies recurring payment. Neither arrangement is automatically superior. The team must investigate purchase preferences, service obligations, refund behavior, update costs, and the cost of reaching customers under each model.

Compare alternatives using consistent accounting periods. A perpetual license receiving $48 today cannot be compared directly with a $6 monthly subscription using only the first month. Build cash and contribution schedules over a stated horizon. Include the possibility of refunds, cancellations, upgrades, and support. Keep uncertain future upgrades out of the base case until evidence supports them. The result may favor a simpler model despite a less exciting revenue headline.

ClimbOS can store competing business models as scenario objects referencing the same customer evidence. The interface should highlight the assumptions that differ, rather than generating two unrelated essays. A model comparison becomes actionable when it names a low-cost test: a pricing interview, an optional maintenance package, a clearly disclosed pilot, or a landing-page comprehension study. Do not change a real customer's contractual terms merely to run an experiment.

## 4. Understand the unit before calculating unit economics

A unit is the object whose revenue and cost relationship you want to understand. It might be an order, account, seat, project, appointment, location, or fulfilled subscription month. Choosing the wrong unit can hide losses. A profitable order does not imply a profitable customer if returns are expensive. A profitable seat does not imply a profitable account when a large customer requires extensive custom support. A profitable project does not imply a profitable agency if unpaid proposals consume the team's time.

Define a unit contract with the unit name, observation period, currency, revenue recognition basis, included costs, and exclusions. Then calculate contribution as revenue attributable to that unit minus the costs that change because the unit exists or is served. Use gross margin and contribution margin consistently but do not assume they mean the same thing across organizations. The business must state its own accounting boundaries and reconcile management metrics with its accounting system.

A hypothetical ecommerce order produces $90 after discounts and expected refunds. Product and packaging cost $37, payment processing costs $3, and fulfillment costs $10. Contribution before acquisition is $40. If incremental acquisition cost is $26, contribution after acquisition is $14. That is different from saying the company has a $14 profit: rent, management, software, and other fixed expenses still need coverage. If the campaign shifts existing customers into paid clicks, the incremental economics may be worse than the attributed economics suggest.

For an AI software product, useful variable costs may include model calls, research services, generated media, email sends, storage operations, and support effort attributable to usage. Not every infrastructure cost is perfectly variable. Allocate shared expenses transparently and show sensitivity when the allocation changes the decision. A heavy user costing far more than the average can make a plan unprofitable even if the average account looks healthy.

ClimbOS should calculate financial results deterministically from typed inputs. A model may explain the formula, but it should not silently choose cost categories, exchange rates, or billing periods. Require explicit confirmation of a unit definition before showing comparisons. Return “insufficient inputs” when key costs are unknown, while offering a scenario range that clearly labels those missing costs as assumptions.

## 5. Contribution, margin targets, and break-even

The basic formulas are straightforward:

- Contribution = net revenue − defined variable costs.
- Contribution margin = contribution / net revenue, when net revenue is positive.
- Break-even units = fixed costs / contribution per unit, when unit contribution is positive; round up when units must be whole.
- Price required for target margin = variable cost / (1 − target margin), under the stated cost model.

The last formula is frequently confused with markup. Adding 30% to a cost of $10 produces a $13 price and a margin of about 23.1%. Achieving a 30% margin requires a price of approximately $14.29. This difference matters when ClimbOS evaluates plans intended to maintain at least a 30% margin. The requirement must specify whether it covers only usage costs, all service delivery costs, or a wider allocation including support and infrastructure.

Suppose a hypothetical ClimbOS plan has $12 in expected monthly model and research expense, $2 in email and infrastructure allocation, and $4 in variable support. The defined service cost is $18. A price of $25 yields $7 contribution, or 28%. A $30 price yields $12, or 40%. These figures are scenarios, not current vendor prices or a recommended public price. They omit taxes, payment fees, refunds, acquisition, and fixed costs unless those are separately added to the model.

Break-even adds another perspective. If monthly fixed expenses are hypothetically $6,000 and each account contributes $12 after the defined service costs, 500 active paying accounts cover those expenses before other omitted items. But this steady-state result says nothing about how long acquisition takes or how much cash funds growth. A business can have attractive steady-state economics and still run out of cash while building its customer base.

Use margin scenarios across usage levels rather than a single average. At minimum, evaluate light, typical, and heavy usage with explicit assumptions. Consider concurrency and retry storms as well as individual request cost. If a plan's promised usage can exceed its sustainable cost envelope, adjust packaging, include fair metering, or change the execution policy before launch. Hiding expensive work behind an unlimited label does not remove its economic consequences.

## 6. Acquisition costs and the danger of convenient denominators

Customer acquisition cost requires both a cost boundary and a customer boundary. Paid media CAC might include only ad spend. Fully loaded CAC might include relevant labor, agencies, creative, sales tooling, and commissions. Both can be useful, but they answer different questions. A channel comparison using ad-only cost for one channel and fully loaded cost for another creates a misleading ranking.

Define the denominator carefully. A signup is not a paying customer. A paying customer who immediately refunds may not qualify as a successful acquisition under the decision being evaluated. For a recurring business, report acquisition cohorts and their subsequent retention. For sales-led businesses, account for the time between lead generation, sales effort, signed agreement, and collected payment. A monthly snapshot may pair today's spend with customers generated by earlier spending.

A hypothetical campaign spends $600 and produces 100 signups, 20 activated accounts, and 5 paying accounts within the declared conversion window. Cost per signup is $6, cost per activation is $30, and ad-only CAC is $120. If sales and creative work add $400, fully loaded acquisition cost for those five customers is $200 each. Neither number establishes incrementality: some customers may have arrived without the campaign. Report the distinction rather than choosing the more flattering metric.

Payback measures how long customer contribution takes to recover acquisition expense. With a stable monthly contribution of $25, a $200 acquisition cost implies eight months under a simplified model. Real cohorts may cancel, expand, receive refunds, or incur higher onboarding costs. A more useful calculation accumulates expected contribution month by month until it covers acquisition. If the cumulative amount never covers it within the modeled horizon, report that explicitly.

ClimbOS should display the denominator beside every cost metric. A card labeled “$30 CAC” must not actually mean cost per activated signup. Preserve channel attribution rules and conversion windows as metadata. When data is too sparse, show counts and intervals or a scenario instead of presenting precision that the underlying evidence cannot support.

## 7. Lifetime value without fictional lifetimes

Lifetime value can be a useful planning concept, but it becomes dangerous when a short observation window is extrapolated into years of certain revenue. Begin with observed contribution over a fixed horizon. Then add an explicit forecast for the unobserved period. Keep those components separate. The owner should see how much of the claimed value is earned and how much depends on assumptions about retention, expansion, prices, or cost.

A cohort-based contribution model can be written as the sum, across future periods, of probability of remaining active multiplied by expected net revenue less expected servicing cost, optionally discounted. Account for different customer segments when behavior differs materially. A simple revenue-divided-by-churn shortcut assumes conditions that may not fit a growing, seasonal, or heterogeneous business. Treat it as a limited approximation, not a law.

Imagine a hypothetical cohort of 100 customers. Across three months, collected revenue is $8,000 and defined service cost is $2,600. Observed contribution is $5,400, or $54 per initial customer. A proposed six-month forecast adds another $30 per initial customer under a retention scenario. Report observed three-month contribution of $54 and forecast additional contribution of $30; do not label the entire $84 as measured lifetime value. If acquisition cost was $65, the observed cohort has not yet paid back.

Use scenario analysis to expose the important uncertainty. Vary retention, expansion, and serving cost independently where plausible. Avoid changing every assumption optimistically at once. If a channel is attractive only under the highest retention case, the next useful task is often to validate retention, not to increase channel spend. The business may also choose a short cash payback requirement even when modeled long-run value is attractive.

In ClimbOS, store observed and forecast contribution in different fields, with cohort definitions and time horizons. Never allow a generated paragraph to erase that distinction. Link every assumption to its owner, rationale, sensitivity, and review date. Book 12 extends the statistical and forecasting methods; the strategic rule here is that uncertainty should change investment size and evidence collection, not disappear inside a ratio.

## 8. Cash, capacity, and the growth ceiling

Cash collection and economic value are related but different. An annual prepayment provides cash now while creating future delivery obligations. A large service contract may recognize revenue while payment arrives later. Inventory may be paid for before customers buy it. Growth increases these timing differences. A business should model a cash schedule alongside contribution so that a profitable plan does not exceed available financing.

Construct a rolling schedule with starting cash, expected collections, committed outflows, discretionary investment, and ending cash. Label uncertain collections rather than treating pipeline as money in the bank. Stress-test delayed payments, refunds, lower conversion, and unexpectedly expensive delivery. A useful investment gate asks whether the business remains above its chosen minimum cash buffer in a plausible downside case. That buffer is an owner policy, not a universal percentage.

Capacity is equally concrete. A service company with 80 available delivery hours cannot responsibly sell 120 hours merely because lead generation is inexpensive. A software team may face support or onboarding bottlenecks even when digital delivery scales. A local provider has geography and scheduling constraints. Model the limiting resource in the same units as demand: consultations, installation slots, shipping capacity, support hours, or concurrent processing jobs.

A hypothetical agency can onboard two new projects per month without harming existing work. A campaign forecasting six project wins is not ready to scale unless the team plans additional capacity or staggers starts. The appropriate marketing strategy may emphasize higher-fit projects, deposits, waitlists, referral partners, or improved qualification. Increasing lead volume could be the least valuable intervention.

ClimbOS should make capacity a first-class constraint in channel planning. Ask owners for a comfortable operating range, then compare proposed demand with that range. When capacity is unknown, show the operational question that must be answered. An AI assistant can summarize bookings and support loads, but the business should approve assumptions about hiring, quality standards, or acceptable customer waiting time.

## 9. Competitive advantage is a system of choices

A differentiator matters only if customers value it, the business can deliver it, and competitors cannot erase its usefulness immediately at acceptable cost. “AI-powered,” “premium,” and “easy to use” are descriptions, not demonstrated advantages. Test the customer consequence: what task becomes easier, what risk falls, what decision becomes possible, or what result arrives sooner? Then examine whether the supporting activities reinforce one another.

Porter's trade-off framework explains why pursuing incompatible positions can undermine strategic coherence. The practical lesson is to connect an attractive promise to operating choices and explicit limits. [Harvard explanation of strategic trade-offs](https://www.isc.hbs.edu/strategy/creating-a-successful-strategy/Pages/making-strategic-trade-offs.aspx). The analysis below applies that idea to original hypothetical situations; it does not reproduce a company case study.

For a software utility focused on privacy-sensitive professionals, an advantage hypothesis might combine local processing, clear data controls, dependable offline behavior, and support documentation that explains those properties. Marketing alone cannot create the position. The product architecture, defaults, pricing, onboarding, and support must support it. If the team later makes remote processing mandatory for a key task, it should revisit the strategic promise rather than quietly retaining the old message.

A low-price position needs its own reinforcing system: limited customization, controlled support expense, efficient self-service, clear scope, and a sustainable distribution model. Offering unlimited bespoke assistance while competing on the lowest price may be a deliberate acquisition investment, but it should not be mistaken for a sustainable model until the economics support it. Strategic coherence requires the team to admit where a good opportunity belongs to a different business model.

Build an advantage review with four tests: customer relevance, delivery evidence, economic compatibility, and durability. Score each with a written rationale, not merely a number. Ask how a competitor could respond and what assets accumulate through repeated delivery. ClimbOS should propose advantage hypotheses and evidence gaps; it should not declare a moat from a homepage claim. Evidence of repeated customer preference is stronger than the founder's choice of adjectives.

## 10. Segment before prioritizing

Segmentation is commercially useful when different groups need different choices. Demographic labels alone may not change the strategy. More useful dimensions include urgency, buying trigger, workflow complexity, implementation burden, risk, available budget, purchase authority, and route to reach the buyer. The same-sized companies may require entirely different sales and product approaches because one has an urgent compliance deadline and another is casually exploring improvements.

Start with a segment definition that a team member can apply consistently. Specify inclusion and exclusion rules, observable signals, the relevant buying situation, and a path to verify those signals. Avoid a segment like “all businesses that want to grow.” A narrower hypothetical segment could be independent service firms with recurring client work, an owner managing sales, and difficulty following up on inbound inquiries. That definition suggests research questions without claiming the segment is already proven.

Evaluate segments using a gate followed by a comparison. The gate rejects segments the business cannot serve lawfully, competently, or economically. The comparison considers problem severity, willingness and ability to pay, reachability, time to value, retention potential, competition, and strategic fit. Weights are explicit decision policies. They do not transform weak evidence into objective truth. Record confidence for each rating and test whether reasonable changes in weights alter the preferred segment.

A small high-fit segment can be a better starting market than a large poorly reachable one. Initial focus allows the team to learn a repeated buying process and build reusable proof. Expansion becomes more credible when the team can state what transfers to the adjacent segment and what must change. An agency moving from local clinics to ecommerce brands may retain design capabilities while changing buyers, sales cycles, compliance considerations, and proof requirements.

ClimbOS should maintain segment-specific assumptions, economics, and evidence. Do not average all accounts into a single ideal customer profile when their costs and retention differ. Let the owner compare the priority segment with a runner-up, including the strongest reason the runner-up could win. This encourages reasoned choice rather than a polished recommendation that conceals alternatives.

## 11. Manage the offer and portfolio

An offer combines an outcome, scope, delivery method, conditions, price, and risk allocation. Two products with similar features can have different offers because one includes implementation, a limited pilot, a migration service, or a clear usage allowance. Before building another feature, investigate whether buyers understand what they receive and what effort they must contribute. Confusion can resemble lack of demand.

Offer design should remove uncertainty honestly. Specify who the offer suits, what is included, what is excluded, how long delivery takes under stated conditions, and what happens when requirements change. Guarantees require operational and financial support. Avoid promises about revenue growth that the provider cannot control. A narrower process guarantee may be feasible where an outcome guarantee is not, but even that needs explicit terms and a clear fulfillment mechanism.

Portfolio decisions compare the opportunity cost of supporting multiple offers. Each additional product or plan creates acquisition, onboarding, documentation, maintenance, and support work. A founder with several applications may gain shared distribution and brand recognition, yet still fragment attention. Calculate which assets truly transfer and which merely share a logo. A common checkout is useful; an unfocused roadmap can still weaken every product.

Use an investment ledger with maintain, improve, test, and retire decisions. Maintain means the product receives enough attention to meet its promises. Improve means a specific constraint merits focused work. Test means an uncertain opportunity receives a bounded experiment. Retire requires a customer transition plan and fulfillment of obligations. Do not let a score automatically remove access or change subscriptions. Portfolio governance affects real customers.

ClimbOS can assist by comparing contribution, demand evidence, delivery burden, and strategic learning across offers. Separate current revenue from future potential, and distinguish customer commitments from discretionary roadmap work. Recommend a manageable number of concurrent growth projects based on actual capacity. The aim is concentrated learning and reliable delivery, not the appearance of a large opportunity pipeline.

## 12. Sequence investment under uncertainty

A growth budget is a portfolio of commitments with different reversibility and learning value. Interviews, prototypes, limited campaigns, annual software contracts, permanent hires, and inventory purchases do not create the same obligations. Invest in evidence before making the commitments that depend on it. This is especially important when the team's confidence comes from enthusiastic feedback rather than observed buying behavior.

A useful sequence moves from problem evidence to offer comprehension, purchase intent, paid adoption, repeat value, and scalable acquisition. The sequence is not perfectly linear. Existing businesses may already have strong evidence in some areas, and sales-led products may need a paid pilot to test several assumptions together. The discipline is to state which assumption the next investment addresses and what result would justify a larger commitment.

Evaluate an experiment using decision value rather than only expected revenue. A small test that rules out an expensive mistake can be valuable even when it produces no customers. Conversely, a campaign producing a handful of customers may teach little if targeting, messaging, and conversion tracking are all changed simultaneously. Design the test so that its outcome informs a specific decision.

For a hypothetical $600 monthly advertising ceiling, do not automatically spend the full amount. If landing-page analytics are unreliable, reserve spending while validating the measurement path. If the product cannot accept payments, use the time to resolve that issue. If a small test reveals a credible acquisition route, retain enough budget to confirm the result rather than scattering money across every platform. A ceiling protects resources; it is not a utilization target.

ClimbOS should label commitments by reversibility, cash timing, and evidence requirements. Forecasts should show ranges and the assumptions driving them. The owner can then decide whether to pay for speed, reduce scope, or collect more evidence. An automated plan should never confuse a positive expected value estimate with certainty that the business can tolerate the downside.

## 13. AI-native opportunities in strategic work

AI can reduce the cost of assembling evidence, comparing alternatives, drafting experiments, and maintaining decision records. Its strongest strategic role is often to make reasoning inspectable and repeated work consistent. It can extract stated prices from approved pages, classify interview excerpts, compare cost scenarios, and identify contradictions between a promised service level and observed capacity. These outputs still need reliable inputs and evaluation.

Separate four activities: extraction, analysis, proposal, and execution. Extraction records what a source says. Analysis explains implications under assumptions. Proposal recommends a choice. Execution changes something outside the analysis environment. Combining all four into one unreviewed response makes it difficult to detect where an error entered. A structured workflow lets ClimbOS validate facts and arithmetic before asking an owner to approve a concrete plan.

For customer research, a model can group interview observations by buying trigger and obstacle, but should preserve the original excerpts and dissenting cases. For economics, it can identify missing inputs and explain a deterministic calculation, but should not invent service costs. For strategy, it can generate competing approaches with trade-offs, but should not treat persuasive language as evidence. For monitoring, it can flag changed conditions that require a new decision rather than automatically rewriting the strategy every day.

Automation becomes more useful when the action boundary is narrow. A weekly internal diagnostic can run with read-only analytics and a bounded compute budget. A pricing change, outbound campaign, or increase in advertising spend requires a different permission scope. Existing owner authorization can cover routine actions within explicit limits, but access to an integration alone does not define those limits. Design permissions around business consequences.

In ClimbOS, store the model version, evidence snapshot, prompt version, calculation version, and reviewer decision for important recommendations. Evaluate whether the assistant finds material constraints, distinguishes facts from hypotheses, and changes its advice when inputs change. The success criterion is better decisions with less owner effort, not a larger number of generated reports.

## 14. A complete hypothetical business diagnostic

Consider an invented subscription product called Northstar Desk. It serves small service teams, charges $40 monthly, and has 80 paying accounts. These are scenario inputs, not verified facts about any real product. The owner wants 20 additional paying accounts in 90 days, can spend at most $600 monthly on ads, and has ten hours weekly for growth work. Defined monthly service costs average $12 per account, with higher support expense during onboarding.

First, calculate the current contribution before acquisition and fixed expenses: 80 × ($40 − $12) = $2,240 monthly. That does not establish company profit. Next, inspect retention and acquisition evidence. Suppose only three months of cohort data exist and some cancellation reasons are missing. Lifetime value is therefore uncertain. Do not justify an aggressive acquisition program using a mature steady-state retention assumption the business has never observed.

Second, diagnose the funnel. Suppose the owner provides records showing that many qualified trials reach setup but do not complete the first useful workflow. Interviews indicate that configuration terminology is confusing. This is evidence for an activation obstacle, but other explanations remain possible: users may lack the underlying data or may have selected the wrong product. Design an onboarding comparison with observed task completion and brief follow-up interviews. Avoid claiming the interface change caused revenue improvement before measuring downstream behavior.

Third, compare strategies. Strategy A increases paid trial volume immediately. Strategy B improves first-value completion and uses existing interested leads for a permissioned re-engagement test. Strategy C sells a high-touch onboarding package. Compare each against cash, support capacity, user preference, and learning value. If the owner cannot deliver the service in C and activation weakness makes A wasteful, B may be the best first experiment. This is a conditional recommendation, not a universal rule.

Finally, define the review. Measure eligible trial count, first-value completion within the stated window, paid conversion, support minutes, refunds, and cohort contribution. Record every numerator and denominator. If task completion improves but paid conversion does not, investigate value and buyer fit instead of declaring success. ClimbOS should preserve this chain from evidence through choice and outcome so future recommendations learn from the actual result.

## 15. Machine-readable diagnostic and investment playbooks

The following contracts are proposed knowledge artifacts. They are valid YAML and JSON examples, not installed integrations. The numerical limits in them are illustrative owner policies. Production adapters must validate identifiers, permissions, cost reservations, and current business constraints before acting.

```yaml
id: b01-business-diagnostic
version: 1
mode: analysis_only
required_inputs:
  - business_snapshot_id
  - goal_definition
  - currency
  - observation_window
  - cost_boundary
  - capacity_definition
steps:
  - id: identity
    instruction: Verify canonical business and separate reported from measured facts.
  - id: mechanism
    instruction: Map buyer, problem, offer, delivery, collection, and repeat value.
  - id: economics
    instruction: Calculate contribution using explicit cost inclusions and exclusions.
  - id: diagnosis
    instruction: Compare demand, activation, retention, capacity, and cash constraints.
  - id: alternatives
    instruction: Produce competing approaches with evidence and trade-offs.
  - id: decision
    instruction: Select a bounded experiment or request material missing evidence.
outputs:
  - diagnosis_with_counterevidence
  - unit_economics_with_assumptions
  - alternatives_and_deferred_work
  - experiment_and_review_date
stop_conditions:
  - unresolved_business_identity
  - inconsistent_currency_or_period
  - missing_authorization_for_external_action
```

```json
{
  "id": "b01-investment-gate",
  "version": 1,
  "status": "proposed_policy",
  "requirements": {
    "positive_unit_contribution": true,
    "cash_downside_reviewed": true,
    "delivery_capacity_reviewed": true,
    "measurement_definition_present": true,
    "explicit_budget_authorization": true
  },
  "outcomes": ["eligible_for_owner_review", "collect_evidence", "reject_with_reason"],
  "missing_input_behavior": "return_unknown_and_name_the_missing_input",
  "execution_permission": "none"
}
```

Use the diagnostic when a business first joins ClimbOS, after a material product change, or when outcomes contradict the current strategy. Do not rerun every component merely because a schedule exists. Refresh the affected assumptions and preserve the previous version. Use the investment gate before scaling a commercial offer; a failing gate should identify a repairable condition rather than produce an unexplained refusal. A deliberately loss-making learning experiment needs a separate, explicit loss ceiling and owner-approved learning objective; it must not be mislabeled as passing the positive-contribution scaling gate.

## 16. Implement the knowledge in ClimbOS

A practical implementation needs business snapshots, segments, offers, evidence claims, unit definitions, cost schedules, capacity records, decisions, experiments, and outcomes. Each record belongs to a tenant and references the relevant version. Avoid storing important economics only inside narrative reports. Structured records allow recalculation when a price or cost changes and help distinguish a stale recommendation from a current one.

The initial owner experience can be simple: choose the goal, confirm the business, add known constraints, and connect or provide evidence. Advanced fields can remain available in a details panel. Progressive disclosure should reduce unnecessary work without hiding uncertainty. If the owner does not know retention, the interface should explain what can still be decided and what requires a cautious scenario.

Implement arithmetic as a small domain library with defined units and missing-value behavior. For margin, reject an invalid target at or above 100% and avoid dividing by zero revenue. For payback, accumulate contribution over time and return an unrecovered result when appropriate. For capacity, compare equivalent time periods. For multicurrency analysis, require an explicit conversion policy and observation date. These are meaningful correctness requirements, not cosmetic checks.

A recommendation record should include its diagnosis, candidate alternatives, evidence references, assumptions, deterministic calculations, proposed actions, cost envelope, dependencies, review date, and invalidation triggers. A changed product price may invalidate acquisition economics without invalidating interview observations. A changed customer segment may require a much broader review. Use this dependency logic to refresh efficiently and preserve historical explanations.

Evaluate the module with realistic cases: profitable but cash-constrained commerce, high-signup low-retention software, a capacity-limited agency, a subscription product with expensive heavy users, and a business with almost no data. Include contradictory evidence and unknown costs. Passing means the system selects appropriate next decisions, labels uncertainty, performs correct arithmetic, and never fabricates business facts to complete a template. The module is useful when an owner can understand and challenge its reasoning, then act on a concrete, affordable experiment.
