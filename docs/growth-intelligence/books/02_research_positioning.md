# Book 02 — Customer Research and Positioning

*ClimbOS Growth Intelligence Library. Research cutoff: October 19, 2026. Frameworks and implementation policies proposed in this book are operating guidance, not measured industry benchmarks. All worked business examples are hypothetical unless explicitly identified as a sourced case.*

## 1. Research should change a decision

Customer research earns its place when it changes what a business builds, promises, sells, or stops doing. A collection of attractive personas is not enough. Before collecting information, name the decision, the alternatives, the consequence of being wrong, and the observation that would change the decision. This turns research from an open-ended content exercise into an investment in reduced uncertainty.

Consider a small software business choosing between independent consultants and operations teams. Interviewing “people interested in productivity” cannot settle this choice. The decision requires information about recurring work, urgency, purchase authority, practical access to customers, and the cost of serving each segment. Write those uncertainties before recruiting. Otherwise the team will collect whichever stories are easiest to hear and call the resulting convenience sample a strategy.

Use a decision brief with five sentences. We need to decide whom to serve first. Our current preference is a stated segment for a stated reason. The most dangerous uncertainty is a specific unproven assumption. We will examine a defined set of observations. We will reconsider if an explicit contrary result appears. The brief should fit on one screen and remain attached to every resulting recommendation.

ClimbOS should begin its research workflow with this brief. A business owner can answer ordinary questions such as “Which customers are you considering?” and “What would be expensive to get wrong?” The system can translate those answers into research questions, but must preserve the owner's original wording. A generated research agenda becomes a proposal rather than an invisible change to the business goal.

## 2. Separate the customer, the user, and the buying situation

A useful segment is a group of purchasing situations that can be served with sufficiently similar product capabilities, messaging, acquisition methods, and economics. Demographic similarity alone does not establish this. Two founders with the same company size may buy for different reasons: one needs a process working tomorrow; the other is replacing a system across several teams after a security review.

Identify the user who experiences the problem, the champion who advances the purchase, the payer who controls funds, the evaluator who checks suitability, and the approver who can stop adoption. One person can fill every role in a small business. In larger organizations the roles can conflict. A daily user may value speed while an approver values auditability. Positioning must acknowledge the buying system without trying to say everything to everyone at once.

Describe a segment using circumstances, frequency of the job, current alternative, buying trigger, consequences of failure, and access route. Add geography or company size when they alter the buying process or eligibility. “Small agencies onboarding several clients each month and manually chasing missing project information” is more operationally useful than “busy professionals aged thirty to forty-five.” It implies research questions, product requirements, and places to recruit participants.

The Jobs to Be Done lens concerns the progress people seek in particular circumstances and includes functional, social, and emotional dimensions. That conceptual foundation comes from the [Christensen Institute's explanation](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/jobs-to-be-done/). The procedure developed here is an application of that lens: reconstruct the situation, identify the existing response, and examine what would make switching worthwhile. It is not a claim that one interview script can reveal every customer's motives.

## 3. Build an evidence ledger before a persona

Research records should distinguish an observation from an interpretation. “The participant opened a spreadsheet and copied six rows” is an observation. “The participant needs automation” is an interpretation. “All agencies need automation” is an unjustified generalization. Keeping these levels separate makes disagreement useful: colleagues can inspect the same observation and offer competing explanations.

For every evidence item, store its origin, collection date, participant or account identifier, consent status where relevant, precise excerpt or event locator, and limitations. An anonymized identifier is usually sufficient for analysis. Store identifying information separately when possible. A published review can support the statement that one reviewer reported a problem; it cannot establish the prevalence of that problem among all customers.

Classify findings as measured, owner-reported, source-reported, assumption, inference, or calculated. These categories align with the ClimbOS engineering brief. A business owner's claim about customers is valuable input but should not silently become measured evidence. Likewise, a competitor's homepage establishes what that competitor publicly claims, rather than proving the claimed outcome occurred.

Create contradiction links. When one account says setup is easy and another abandons setup, the disagreement is information. Differences in task complexity, prior experience, permissions, or implementation support may explain it. Do not average contradictory statements into a bland conclusion. Preserve the conditional structure: setup appeared easy for experienced solo users and difficult for administrators importing historical records.

## 4. Choose methods according to uncertainty

Use interviews to explore a buying story, observation to understand actual work, usability sessions to identify interaction barriers, surveys to estimate patterns within a known sampling frame, and commercial experiments to test behavior under an offer. None of these methods substitutes for all the others. A survey of newsletter subscribers may characterize subscribers while saying little about buyers who never join the newsletter.

When the uncertainty is whether a problem exists in practice, observe or reconstruct recent incidents. When the uncertainty is which words people understand, test comprehension. When the uncertainty is willingness to pay, seek a realistic commercial commitment with clear terms. When the uncertainty is implementation burden, run an assisted pilot and record every manual intervention. Match the method to the failure you are trying to avoid.

Start with the cheapest method that can provide decision-changing evidence. That may be reviewing existing support conversations before commissioning new interviews. However, support data overrepresents people who contacted support. Pair it with evidence from successful quiet customers and people who left. The point is not a ritual of “triangulation” but deliberate exposure to groups that the first dataset misses.

Set a stop rule before collecting. For exploratory interviews, a proposed rule might be to pause after two successive sessions fail to add a new decision-relevant buying barrier, while ensuring each chosen participant category is represented. This is a practical scheduling policy, not proof of statistical saturation. If the decision has high financial consequences or major subgroup differences, collect more evidence or explicitly narrow the conclusion.

## 5. Recruit for contrast rather than applause

Recruit recent buyers, unsuccessful evaluators, people who chose an alternative, and customers who stopped using the product. Separate those categories in the analysis. A happy customer's explanation of a past purchase may be useful, but it does not reveal all the barriers faced by someone who never adopted. Recruiting only enthusiasts creates a particularly persuasive form of selection bias.

Use neutral invitations. State that the purpose is to understand how the person handled a particular task, that criticism is useful, and that the session is not a sales presentation. If compensation is offered, make it independent of positive feedback. Do not imply that participation guarantees access to unreleased capabilities. Any recording arrangement should be explicit before recording begins.

For a small business without a research panel, begin with authorized existing relationships, opt-in interview requests, relevant communities that permit research recruitment, and introductions. ClimbOS must not pretend it owns a prospect database. It can help write recruitment materials and organize owner-supplied contacts, but sending messages requires the appropriate recipient, channel, and authorization checks. A generated list of plausible people is not a verified contact list.

Use a recruitment matrix rather than a rigid quota presented as science. For example, aim initially to hear from several recent buyers, several nonbuyers, and several former users, then adjust based on contradictions. Record who was invited, who accepted, who declined, and why when voluntarily disclosed. Nonresponse does not reveal a motive, but knowing its extent prevents the team from describing respondents as the entire market.

## 6. Conduct interviews around an actual episode

Begin with a recent concrete event: “Tell me about the last time you prepared a client handoff.” Ask what happened first, what triggered the task, who became involved, which tools appeared, what went wrong, and what the person did next. Follow the sequence before asking for general opinions. The sequence often reveals constraints that disappear in a polished explanation of how work is supposed to happen.

Ask for artifacts when participants can share them appropriately: a blank template, a redacted checklist, or a demonstration with fictional data. Never require private client information simply to make an interview vivid. Observe transitions between tools. A complaint about “too many spreadsheets” might actually be a complaint about uncertain responsibility, duplicate entry, or waiting for approvals. Each implies a different solution.

Separate past behavior from future intention. “I would definitely use this” is weaker commercial evidence than “we paid an employee to solve this last week,” although neither guarantees a future purchase. Ask about the last expenditure, the approval conversation, and the alternative considered. Avoid defending the product when the participant misunderstands it; misunderstanding is itself useful evidence for positioning.

End by asking what would make switching unacceptable, who else should be consulted, and what important question was missed. Then summarize the story back in neutral language. Let the participant correct it. Keep any product demonstration until after the discovery portion, and mark statements collected after exposure to the demonstration so they are not mistaken for unprompted needs.

## 7. Turn interview material into decision evidence

Analyze interviews in two passes. First extract concrete facts, quotations, events, and reported constraints without forcing them into a preferred framework. Then interpret patterns across cases. This order reduces the temptation to make every participant confirm the category the team hoped to find. A short, accurate event summary is often more useful than pages of sentiment labels.

Create a codebook with definitions and counterexamples. “Urgency” might require a specific deadline or visible consequence of delay; enthusiastic adjectives alone do not qualify. “Switching barrier” might include data migration, stakeholder approval, lost integrations, or fear of interruption. If multiple analysts apply the same code differently, revise the definition and revisit a sample rather than concealing inconsistency behind a combined chart.

Count participants and accounts separately. Five interviews from one company do not provide five independent company buying decisions. Preserve denominators when describing findings: “four of seven interviewed accounts reported manual reconciliation” is inspectable. “Most companies struggle with reconciliation” is broader than the evidence. Qualitative counts help organize a sample; they do not automatically estimate market prevalence.

Maintain negative cases. If the proposed segment values a workflow but one participant rejects it, explain why. Perhaps the person's existing system is sufficient or the pain occurs too rarely. These exceptions can define eligibility better than another enthusiastic endorsement. A precise exclusion can save more acquisition money than a broad promise attracts.

## 8. Assess problem importance without false precision

Use an importance profile rather than one magic score. Examine frequency, consequence, current effort, budget relevance, urgency, and access to authority. Score each on a small, documented scale only to support comparison. A score should summarize evidence, never replace it. Unknown values should remain unknown rather than becoming a convenient midpoint.

One proposed comparison formula is `priority = severity × recurrence × readiness × confidence / delivery_effort`. Each factor must use a stated scale and the same definitions across candidates. This formula is a prioritization heuristic, not a statistical model. Multiplication makes weak readiness consequential: a severe problem with no route to purchase may be a poor immediate target despite long-term importance.

For a hypothetical agency task, severity is four out of five, recurrence four, readiness three, confidence 0.6, and delivery effort three. The resulting index is 9.6. Another segment scores severity five, recurrence two, readiness one, confidence 0.4, and effort four, producing 1.0. The difference suggests where to investigate first; it does not predict nine times more revenue.

Supplement the index with a written reason against the choice. Perhaps the attractive segment requires a missing integration or unusually intensive support. If one prerequisite makes the offer impossible, use an eligibility gate before scoring. A business that cannot legally or technically serve a segment should not let an impressive numerical ranking override that constraint.

## 9. Map alternatives customers actually use

The competitive set includes specialist tools, general tools, internal processes, outsourced services, and doing nothing. Ask which alternative was used in the last real episode. A customer who handles a task through email is not necessarily evaluating a category of software. Their comparison may be between tolerated inconvenience and a new administrative responsibility.

Build an alternatives matrix with the job, current method, reason chosen, frustration, switching cost, and evidence source. Add public competitor facts only after checking their own current documentation. Record dates because prices and feature availability change. Avoid declaring a competitor deficient because a page did not mention a capability. Absence from one retrieved page is not proof of absence from the product.

Distinguish parity requirements from differentiators. A capability may be essential for eligibility yet provide little reason to switch because every viable alternative has it. Conversely, a distinctive feature is commercially irrelevant if the target segment does not value it. Positioning requires a meaningful difference in the chosen comparison frame, not simply a longer feature list.

Use a “why stay?” analysis alongside “why buy?” The existing method may be familiar, approved, already paid for, and integrated into habits. A replacement must overcome that accumulated convenience. Sometimes the right offer is an add-on that reduces one painful step, rather than an ambitious all-in-one replacement. Research should leave that strategic option open.

## 10. Construct a defensible position

A position connects a specific customer situation to a valuable outcome through a credible mechanism, relative to the alternatives that customer considers. Write it internally as: for a defined segment facing a defined circumstance, our offer helps achieve a defined outcome through a stated mechanism; compared with the current alternative, the relevant difference is a verifiable distinction.

The internal statement is not necessarily homepage copy. It is a reasoning tool. Every clause should have either evidence or a hypothesis label. If the mechanism is still proposed, do not publish it as an existing feature. If the outcome depends on customer effort, say so when constructing the offer. A claim that omits a decisive condition can be persuasive and misleading at the same time.

Strategyzer's [Value Proposition Canvas](https://www.strategyzer.com/library/the-value-proposition-canvas) separates a customer profile of jobs, pains, and gains from the offer that addresses them. Use that established framework as a diagnostic: identify important needs and explain how the offer meets them. The additional position contract here adds evidence links, alternative comparisons, and claim approval to make the reasoning executable inside ClimbOS.

Choose a category customers can recognize unless there is strong evidence that an unfamiliar category improves comprehension. A novel category can create explanation costs. When the product spans categories, lead with the buying situation rather than listing every function. A position is a choice about which value to make easiest to understand first.

## 11. Translate the position into a message hierarchy

Build messages in layers. The first layer states the situation and outcome. The second explains the mechanism. The third offers proof and answers the main objection. The final layer supplies detailed capabilities and operational conditions. This structure lets a hurried visitor understand relevance while allowing a serious evaluator to inspect support.

For a hypothetical client-intake tool, an initial message might be “Start projects with the information your team needs.” A supporting sentence could describe a verified collection and review workflow if one exists. If no such capability has been verified, the sentence stays in a concept-testing document. Do not transfer illustrative copy into a real business campaign without checking actual product facts.

Create a claims register. Each claim needs exact wording, support, scope, expiration or review date, and approval status. “Save hours every week” requires evidence about hours, tasks, users, and the observation period. Without such evidence, communicate the mechanism or intended benefit in bounded terms. Evidence should improve the promise, not appear as a footnote attached to an otherwise unsupported headline.

Different buying roles may need different supporting proof while sharing one core position. The daily user needs to see the task completed. The payer needs a plausible economic case. The approver needs appropriate controls. Create role-specific sections or assets instead of forcing every concern into a single overloaded hero paragraph.

## 12. Test comprehension before persuasion

A message test should first ask whether the reader understands the offer. Show a page or short statement, remove it, and ask what the product does, who it is for, and what the next action would achieve. Do not explain missing meaning during the test. Confusion at this stage makes click-through comparisons difficult to interpret because different readers may be responding to different imagined products.

Then test relevance and credibility. Ask which situation the message reminds the participant of, what seems useful, what sounds doubtful, and what would need verification before proceeding. These questions identify both attraction and friction. Asking only “Which headline do you like?” produces a preference contest that may reward novelty rather than commercial clarity.

Use behavioral tests when the offer is sufficiently real. A small landing-page experiment can compare qualified inquiries, completed onboarding, or another meaningful action. Keep traffic source, eligibility, and follow-up comparable. Clicks alone can select a provocative message that attracts the wrong audience. Define the downstream quality measure before running the test.

Do not announce a winner from a handful of conversions. With small samples, report counts, uncertainty, and alternative explanations. A practical decision may still be necessary, but it should be framed as provisional. ClimbOS can recommend the clearer option while stating that commercial superiority remains unproven and scheduling a review after a defined evidence window.

## 13. Work a complete hypothetical example

Imagine a fictional software company considering an intake product for independent consultants and small agencies. This is not a statement about Formkit's actual capabilities or market. The owner has ten research conversations: four consultants, four agencies, and two former users of another tool. The sample was recruited through existing relationships and therefore cannot estimate either market's prevalence.

The consultant stories emphasize occasional confusion and a desire to look professional. Agency stories emphasize repeated handoffs between sales and delivery. Three agencies show recent redacted checklists; two describe project delays after missing information. One agency is satisfied with its existing system and would not migrate. The strongest pattern is not “everyone wants better forms,” but a specific handoff problem under repeated delivery pressure.

The initial choice is to investigate agencies that onboard several projects and have separate sales and delivery responsibilities. The evidence against this choice is implementation complexity: two agencies require integrations the fictional product lacks. The position therefore narrows further to teams whose current approved workflow can accept a lightweight additional collection step. This exclusion makes the pilot feasible.

The pilot offer proposes one onboarding workflow, assisted setup, and a review after three completed handoffs. Hypothetical evaluation criteria are less missing information at delivery, time spent chasing inputs, and whether the delivery lead chooses to continue. No claim of revenue improvement is made. If setup consumes more staff time than it saves during the observed period, the team revisits the workflow or target rather than celebrating signups.

## 14. Estimate economic value carefully

A simple time-value model is `monthly_capacity_value = eligible_events × minutes_saved_per_event / 60 × loaded_hourly_cost`. Use compatible units and identify every input as measured or assumed. This calculation estimates released capacity under its assumptions. It does not automatically equal cash savings, profit, or incremental revenue. A salaried employee saving time may simply have more capacity for other work.

Suppose, hypothetically, a team handles twenty eligible handoffs monthly, saves twelve minutes each, and values staff capacity at thirty dollars per hour. The calculation gives one hundred twenty dollars of monthly capacity value. If setup requires four hours, include that cost and the period over which it is assessed. The product's subscription is only one part of the switching decision.

For avoided errors, use `expected_avoided_cost = event_volume × change_in_error_probability × average_consequence_cost`. The change in probability needs credible evidence. With no baseline, present low, middle, and high assumptions instead of a single confident value. Avoid double-counting time spent fixing errors if that time is already included in the time-saving estimate.

Ask what would make the benefit realizable. Saving time in a task that is not a bottleneck may have little effect on throughput. An attractive theoretical return can fail because the buyer lacks implementation capacity. Use the model to identify sensitive assumptions and ask better questions, rather than presenting a precise-looking return as a guaranteed outcome.

## 15. Use AI to accelerate analysis without fabricating customers

AI can propose interview questions, summarize authorized transcripts, suggest codes, identify contradictions, and draft candidate positions. Each output should remain linked to original evidence. The highest-value automation is often organizing messy material so a human can inspect it quickly. Automated confidence phrasing cannot substitute for traceable observations.

Treat a generated persona as a hypothesis document. It is not a research participant. Simulated answers can help rehearse a script or reveal assumptions in a concept, but they must not enter a customer-evidence denominator. Keep synthetic material in a separate namespace and display its status prominently. A plausible quotation invented by a model is still invented.

Create an extraction task that requests episode, trigger, alternative, constraint, quote locator, and uncertainty. Require `null` when the transcript does not answer a field. Follow with deterministic checks that the cited locator exists and that the output did not introduce a capability absent from the business snapshot. A second review can inspect a sample for fidelity before scaled processing.

OpenAI's [Structured Outputs announcement](https://openai.com/index/introducing-structured-outputs-in-the-api/) describes schema-constrained output. In this workflow, that capability can help produce consistent records. Schema validity does not establish factual accuracy. ClimbOS still needs evidence resolution, contradiction handling, authorization boundaries, and evaluation of extraction errors. Model choice should follow the existing OpenAI-first engine and repository evidence rather than a new provider assumption.

## 16. Research playbook: episode discovery

The following is an illustrative configuration contract. Identifiers name fixture evidence, not real customers. Thresholds are proposed operational policies that should be changed when the decision or risk changes.

```yaml
playbook_id: customer_episode_discovery_v1
status: proposed
objective: choose_a_feasible_initial_segment
inputs:
  business_snapshot_id: fixture_snapshot_01
  decision_id: fixture_segment_decision_01
  authorized_dataset_ids: []
  evidence_required: true
recruitment:
  categories:
    - recent_buyer
    - evaluated_without_buying
    - former_user
  contact_database_available: false
  sending_authorization_required: true
interview:
  start_with: most_recent_relevant_episode
  extract:
    - trigger
    - sequence
    - existing_alternative
    - buying_roles
    - switching_barriers
    - concrete_consequences
  prohibited:
    - invented_quotes
    - synthetic_participants_as_evidence
analysis:
  unit_of_comparison: account_buying_situation
  retain_negative_cases: true
  unknown_value: null
output:
  evidence_ledger: required
  candidate_segments: required
  contradictions: required
  decision_recommendation: provisional
execution:
  publish: false
  send_messages: false
  spend_money: false
```

To implement this playbook, validate the snapshot and authorized datasets first. Create recruitment tasks only for sources actually available. Store each transcript or note under an account-scoped research record. Run extraction, then present a review screen with the extracted claim beside its source passage. A reviewer can accept, revise, reject, or mark it unresolved.

The decision output should include the recommended segment, the strongest evidence for it, the strongest evidence against it, and the next observation that could reverse the recommendation. If the evidence does not distinguish candidates, say so. A useful research engine can return “not yet distinguishable” and prescribe a narrower experiment instead of manufacturing a winner.

```json
  ],

  "unknown_policy": "Do not replace missing evidence with a score",
  "output": {
    "decision": "provisional",
    "approved_for_publication": false,
    "required_evidence_ids": [],
    "reasons_against": [],

  }

```

This contract makes publication a distinct state. A position can be analytically promising while its public claims remain unapproved. That separation matters because strategy generation is not authorization to publish or send. A future execution service should accept approved exact claim versions, not a vague assurance that a strategy document was reviewed sometime earlier.

The evaluation should also compare a candidate against the current position. Repositioning has costs: existing customers may be confused, supporting pages may conflict, and sales materials may need revision. Require a migration inventory before changing the public story. The purpose of testing is to improve commercial understanding, not to generate perpetual headline churn.

## 18. A decision tree for research and positioning

If business identity or product facts are uncertain, verify the canonical domain and current offer before proposing a position. If identity is known but the customer problem is vague, reconstruct recent episodes. If the problem is concrete but purchasing behavior differs across accounts, segment by circumstance and buying process. If the segment is clear but the mechanism is unverified, run a capability or workflow check.

If the mechanism is credible but people misunderstand it, test simpler category language and message order. If people understand the promise but do not care, reconsider relevance or urgency before changing button colors. If people care but will not switch, investigate implementation cost, risk, authority, and the existing alternative. If people try and leave, examine delivered value rather than assuming the acquisition message needs more intensity.

When evidence conflicts, first ask whether the records concern the same job, customer type, period, and product version. If they do not, retain separate conditional conclusions. If they do, collect a discriminating observation. Do not solve disagreement by counting more weak opinions against fewer strong behavioral records without considering what each record can actually establish.

Stop research when the remaining uncertainty is unlikely to change the next affordable decision. Continue when the uncertainty could cause a consequential irreversible choice. This is a judgment about decision exposure and information value, not a universal number of interviews. Keep the stopping rationale visible in the research record.

## 19. Implement the knowledge inside ClimbOS

Add proposed domain entities for research decisions, participants, evidence items, episode summaries, segment hypotheses, position versions, claim approvals, and evaluation runs. Reuse existing schema and authorization conventions after repository inspection. Convex should hold tenant-scoped state and workflow progress; the Vercel application should expose review, comparison, and approval surfaces. These are recommendations, not claims about functionality already shipped.

Store evidence separately from interpretations so one source can support several hypotheses without being duplicated. An interpretation should reference source identifiers and the exact snapshot used. A new product price, capability change, or contradictory customer record should invalidate affected claims for review. Historical positions must remain explainable even after the current recommendation changes.

The default owner interface should show three things: who the current recommendation serves, why that choice is plausible, and what remains uncertain. Advanced views can expose sampling, coding, score assumptions, and calculations. Use ordinary language in the main view; do not require a nontechnical owner to understand an embedding index before approving a research conclusion.

Resend can be considered for authorized research invitations and follow-up messages where supported by actual implementation and sending policies. It should not become an assumed cold-outreach engine. Keep suppression and contact permissions separate from analytical interest. A person being an attractive interview candidate does not establish that their address is available or that an invitation is authorized.

## 20. Evaluate research quality and maintain the system

Build a frozen evaluation set containing clear episodes, ambiguous episodes, contradictory records, missing fields, synthetic fixtures, and adversarial source text. Check whether the system extracts only supported details, preserves uncertainty, identifies buying roles correctly, and keeps synthetic examples out of evidence. Include examples where the correct recommendation is to defer judgment.

Measure decision usefulness with human review. Can an owner understand the segment? Can a reviewer inspect the supporting evidence? Does the next experiment discriminate between competing explanations? Track unsupported claim rate and evidence-locator accuracy alongside cost and latency. A long report or a large number of citations is not sufficient evidence of quality.

Review research when a major product capability changes, a segment repeatedly fails onboarding, a new alternative changes buying criteria, or current evidence becomes stale. Do not refresh everything on a fixed schedule without considering materiality. Stable conceptual frameworks may remain useful while competitor prices or platform permissions need immediate verification.

The completed operating loop is decision, evidence, interpretation, position, test, and revision. Its commercial value comes from disciplined choices. ClimbOS should help owners make those choices with less effort while preserving the difference between a compelling story and a supported conclusion.

## 21. Run a two-week positioning sprint

A short research sprint needs a bounded question and protected analysis time. On the first day, write the decision brief, inventory existing evidence, and identify the most consequential gaps. On the second day, prepare recruitment and a neutral episode script. Keep the first sessions exploratory. If every question assumes the product is the answer, rewrite the script before recruiting more people.

During the middle of the sprint, alternate collection with analysis. After each session, capture an episode summary, one surprising observation, one contradiction, and one implication that remains tentative. This small discipline prevents a final-day synthesis from relying on whichever interview was most memorable. Revise recruitment if an important buying role or a contrasting case is absent, but record the change so the final sample can be understood.

At the end of the first week, create two candidate positions and an explicit status-quo alternative. Describe what each would require the company to be good at. One may demand fast self-service setup; another may require expert implementation support. Positioning is partly an operating commitment. A message that attracts customers the business cannot serve is a weak strategy even when the words test well.

In the second week, test comprehension, check capability claims, and run the smallest feasible workflow demonstration. Review the alternatives using the same criteria. Document why the preferred position wins provisionally, what evidence is still missing, and which customer situations should be excluded from the initial offer. Finish with an implementation backlog covering product gaps, page changes, sales explanations, measurement, and the next commercial test.

The final sprint artifact should remain compact enough to use. A position statement, evidence map, objections list, claims register, and experiment card are sufficient when each links to deeper records. Avoid turning a useful research result into a large presentation nobody consults. In ClimbOS, these artifacts should be connected views over the same versioned data rather than separately generated documents that can contradict one another.

A sprint fails constructively when it shows that the original choice cannot yet be made. For example, available interviews may describe users while omitting buyers, or the preferred promise may depend on an unbuilt capability. Mark those gaps as blockers and propose the next focused observation. Do not reward the system for generating a polished position regardless of evidence. Reward it for reducing the risk of a bad commercial commitment.
