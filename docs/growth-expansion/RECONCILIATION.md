# Reconcile advice before recommending an action

This is original ClimbOS implementation guidance. Source IDs BK01–BK06 refer to the uploaded-book registry. Article and video records use their own IDs. The rules below are proposed synthesis, not a claim that an author endorses ClimbOS or that the rules have been commercially validated.

## 1. Research price early; do not treat hypothetical promises as purchases

*Monetizing Innovation* motivates early investigation of value and willingness to pay. *The Mom Test* emphasizes learning from actual circumstances and behavior instead of compliments and hypothetical approval. These ideas are compatible when the engine keeps evidence types separate. Ask how a problem is handled now, what it costs, who controls the budget, and how realistic alternatives are evaluated. Then investigate concrete package choices and feasible commitments. Stated price preferences can inform hypotheses without becoming measured revenue.

Proposed branch: if price evidence is only stated, label it as such and choose a realistic next test. If there is a paid pilot, preserve its scope, refund terms, manual work, and selection bias. If observed willingness is below sustainable cost, revisit costs, packaging, customer value, segment, or viability. Raising a spreadsheet price to the sustainable floor does not establish that buyers will accept it. [BK02; BK05]

## 2. Start from best-fit customers when they exist; preserve uncertainty when they do not

*Obviously Awesome* uses evidence about customers who fit the product well to inform positioning. An early product may have no such customers. Do not fabricate a best-customer persona to complete a workflow. Start with provisional alternatives, verified capabilities, potential value, and a candidate buying situation. Research and test these relationships. The position remains a hypothesis until customers understand and prefer it in a relevant commercial context.

Proposed branch: established product → examine differentiated adoption, retention, buying triggers, and service cost across actual customers. Pre-customer product → use exploratory discovery and pilot evidence, clearly marked provisional. Neither branch justifies an unsupported claim of category leadership. [BK03; BK05]

## 3. A growth goal is not a strategy

A goal describes a desired result. Rumelt's strategic kernel provides a way to relate diagnosis, guiding policy, and coherent action. Channel playbooks describe ways to act, but cannot replace diagnosis. An engine that selects a popular channel before identifying the business obstacle may optimize the wrong part of the system.

Proposed branch: identify evidence for the constraint; name a competing explanation; choose an approach; derive actions; record what will be deferred. When the diagnosis is weak, choose a learning step rather than pretending the tactic has become a strategy. Several actions can form one coherent strategy if they reinforce the same approach. A long task list with conflicting operating commitments cannot pass merely because it covers every marketing channel. [BK01; article growth-system guidance]

## 4. Loops and bottlenecks describe different aspects of the same system

Growth-loop material describes how outputs can feed future inputs. Chain-link reasoning identifies where a complementary system is constrained. A product can have a plausible referral loop and still have an activation problem that prevents the loop from functioning. Conversely, improving every small conversion independently can miss the larger mechanism that generates repeated demand.

Proposed branch: map the hypothesized loop and its required transitions, then identify the weakest commercially relevant transition using evidence. Improve the constraint and remeasure the complete mechanism. Do not assume a loop compounds indefinitely; fatigue, saturation, retention, distribution rules, and capacity can limit it. Record cycle time and cost as well as volume. [BK01; Reforge article records]

## 5. The cheapest experiment is not always the best decision investment

*Testing Business Ideas* connects experiments to business assumptions and learning. *Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments* addresses the design and interpretation of controlled evidence. A cheap interview can be appropriate for discovering a problem but cannot establish causal conversion uplift. An expensive randomized experiment may be wasteful if users do not understand the proposition or traffic is too sparse.

Proposed branch: specify the decision, the uncertain assumption, and the evidence needed to distinguish options. Choose a feasible method whose result can change that decision. Compare cost, delay, reversibility, ethical and operational consequences, and evidential limitations. Avoid a universal hierarchy where every question must end in a randomized test, or every small survey counts as validation. [BK04; BK06]

## 6. Statistically significant does not mean commercially worthwhile

A small effect can be measured precisely and remain economically unattractive. A valuable-looking effect can be too uncertain to justify scaling. A directionally positive estimate is not a proven result, and failure to reject a null hypothesis does not establish equivalence.

Proposed branch: define the business-relevant effect, feasible sample, analysis method, observation horizon, costs, and guardrails before collecting results. Investigate assignment and logging failures before interpreting effects. Translate a credible effect into contribution and capacity scenarios with uncertainty preserved. If the test is inconclusive, report the practical decision options rather than renaming the preferred variant the winner. [BK06; BK02]

## 7. Small businesses need a learning path even when A/B tests are infeasible

Controlled-experiment knowledge is important, but advice from high-volume organizations cannot be transferred as if a local service has millions of eligible users. Sparse conversion volume may make a multi-arm test impractical. That does not prohibit useful research.

Proposed branch: use interviews, task observation, comprehension studies, assisted pilots, operational diagnostics, or a larger deliberately chosen change when appropriate. Treat resulting evidence according to its actual strength. A before-after result can reveal an operational problem without proving causal revenue impact. State when the evidence is useful for choosing a bounded next step and insufficient for claiming an incremental effect. [BK04; BK05; BK06]

## 8. Growth and price integrity do not forbid changing the offer

Premature discounting can conceal a positioning, service, or implementation problem. Refusing all changes because a book advocates price discipline can be equally unhelpful. The relevant distinction is between a reactive unexamined price cut and an evidence-backed revision of the offer or business model.

Proposed branch: investigate loss reasons, perceived value, buyer fit, implementation burden, delivery quality, and cost before repricing. Compare scope changes, package redesign, payment timing, service assistance, and a price change. Model net contribution and future obligations. Document the hypothesis and protect existing customer commitments. Do not assume psychological pricing repairs a product that lacks a valuable use case. [BK02; BK03; BK01]

## 9. A successful concierge pilot does not prove scalable automation

Manual delivery can reveal whether a result is valuable and what customers need to achieve it. That evidence is commercially useful. It does not demonstrate that the same result can be delivered automatically, at the promised reliability, with sustainable cost.

Proposed branch: record manual interventions, expertise, support minutes, exceptions, and customer disclosures during the pilot. Maintain separate assumptions for customer demand and automated feasibility. Before scaling, test the actual automation boundary and failure behavior. Do not market a human-produced result as an existing autonomous capability. [BK04; BK02]

## 10. Published success stories need transfer conditions

The public case collection distinguishes reported randomized evidence, observational narratives, and null or failed tests. A source can accurately report what happened without establishing what would happen for another business. Vendor and company narratives can omit failed attempts, costs, population details, or uncertainty.

Proposed branch: extract a mechanism hypothesis, identify population and intervention differences, inspect what was measured, and define a small relevant test. Do not import another company's uplift into a forecast. Repeated sources describing the same event are not independent replications. A null result means that a difference was not established under the source's method; it does not necessarily prove the interventions are identical. [BK06; public-case registry]

## 11. Current execution rules and historical teaching have different jobs

A book or older video may explain durable customer behavior while describing platform tools that have since changed. Current provider documentation governs actual capability and eligibility. Conversely, a current advertising help page is not independent proof that paid acquisition is the best strategy for a particular business.

Proposed branch: use the books and educational material to reason about the decision; use current official sources and installed integration capabilities to validate action feasibility. Store publication time, retrieval time, effective time when known, and verification status separately. A blocked retrieval remains blocked. Do not let a model infer a missing permission or feature from a similar product. [Platform rule records; BK01]

## 12. Knowledge retrieval must preserve applicability and uncertainty

Adding sources does not guarantee the engine retrieves the right exception. A positioning summary without its pre-customer caveat can produce fictional customer evidence. A statistical summary without randomization-unit assumptions can produce invalid analysis. A playbook without provider eligibility can prepare an impossible action.

Proposed branch: index the decision, inputs, applicability, exclusions, source references, freshness class, and proposed policy status alongside each knowledge item. Retrieve the exception with the rule. Keep tenant-private business observations separate from shared reference material. Use the synthetic cases to test whether the engine changes its recommendation when constraints change. This is an implementation proposal requiring evaluation, not an observed result. [Article context/evaluation guidance; BK06]

## Proposed selection contract

Use the most specific applicable rule that is supported by the business evidence and compatible with current execution constraints. If two sources still disagree, retain both, name the disagreement, and recommend a bounded way to resolve it. Avoid silently averaging contradictory advice or presenting a preferred author as a universal authority.

The engine should return: decision; evidence; unresolved assumptions; chosen framework and why; alternatives; proposed action; prerequisites; economic model; measurement; stop/review conditions; and scope of authorization required for any external effect. These fields belong in trusted application contracts and an inspectable owner interface, not only inside prose prompts.
