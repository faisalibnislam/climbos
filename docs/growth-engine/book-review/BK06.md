## 2. Gap notes (original wording, ClimbOS-oriented)

### Ch 3 Twyman's Law and Experimentation Trustworthiness (`index_split_038`–`059`)

- **Core stance:** treat a surprising result as a likely bug until it is checked. Run "assert"-style integrity checks before celebrating (038).
- **Power** (040): not significant ≠ no effect. Define practical significance first, then make sure power is enough for it. If only a subset is affected, analyse that subset, because a diluted effect disappears in the total.
- **p-value misreadings** (041): a p-value is conditioned on the null. It is not the probability that the null is true, and not the false-positive probability. If the analysis was chosen after looking at results, its assumptions are broken.
- **Multiple tests** (043): many metrics, repeated peeks, segments and reruns all inflate false wins. The book points to false discovery rate control.
- **CIs** (044): overlapping per-arm CIs do **not** mean "not significant". Compute the CI of the difference. A single 95% CI does not have a 95% chance of containing the truth.
- **SUTVA** (046): users affecting each other breaks the analysis. Examples: networks, co-editing, two-sided markets, shared resources.
- **Survivorship** (047): analysing only users who stayed for N weeks biases results.
- **Intention-to-treat** (048): analyse by assignment (who was *offered*), not by who took up the offer. Analysing takers only usually inflates the effect. The book notes this matters for e-mail and display ads.
- **SRM** (049): with large samples, a ratio outside roughly ±1% of design is serious. The system should warn and hide reports when the SRM p-value is very low (around 0.001). Causes include redirects, lossy beacons, carry-over from restarts, bad hashing, triggering on attributes the treatment changes (e.g. an "inactive 3 months" e-mail campaign), **sending e-mails to control and treatment at different times of day**, and bot filters that react to treatment. A small imbalance can flip the sign of the result.
- **External validity** (050–053): populations differ, so rerun rather than assume transfer. Over time, **primacy** (adoption lag) and **novelty** (an effect that fades) apply. Detect them by plotting the effect over time and by following the users seen in the first day or two. Gimmicky "ploys" decay quickly.
- **Segments** (054–057): segments are useful for finding bugs, but correct for multiple testing. Segments the treatment can change (feature users vs non-users) mislead because users migrate between them. Use pre-experiment attributes or the aggregate.
- **Simpson's paradox** (058): pooling periods or regions that had different allocation percentages can reverse the sign. This matters for ramp-ups.

### Ch 6 Organizational Metrics (`078`–`085`)

- **Taxonomy:** goal (true north, slow), driver (leading, sensitive, a causal hypothesis), guardrail (business-protecting, and separately trust guardrails). Other types: asset vs engagement, business vs operational, data quality, diagnostic.
- **Principles:** goal metrics should be simple and stable. Drivers should be aligned, actionable, sensitive and hard to game.
- **Techniques:** build quality into the metric (a "good" signup is one that engages). Measuring dissatisfaction is sometimes easier. Every metric is a proxy, so add counter-metrics against clickbait.
- **Validating driver→goal links:** triangulate with surveys/UER, observational data (good for *invalidating*), others' published studies, metric-validation experiments, and a corpus of past experiments.
- **Decompose revenue** into a purchase indicator and conditional revenue. They tell different stories.
- **Guardrail sidebar:** guardrails are sensitive things most teams shouldn't move (latency, errors, crashes, revenue indicator). Indicator or capped versions have lower variance.
- **Gameability sidebar:** measure user value and actions, not vanity counts. Unconstrained short-term revenue is gameable (price hikes, ad clutter). LTV is the guiding principle.

### Ch 7 Metrics for Experimentation and the OEC (`086`–`091`)

- Experiment metrics must be measurable in the test window, **attributable** to a variant (third-party data often isn't), and sensitive and timely. Use surrogates for slow outcomes like annual renewal, and truncate outliers.
- The OEC is a weighted combination of key metrics. It aligns decisions and enables automated shipping.
- **Interim four-way decision rule:** ship if all key metrics are flat or positive with ≥1 positive. Don't ship if all are flat or negative with ≥1 negative. All flat → don't ship (add power, fail fast or pivot). Mixed → explicit trade-off, and later learn weights.
- **Keep key metrics to about 5.** With k independent metrics at α = 0.05, P(at least one false positive) = 1 − 0.95^k: about 23% at k = 5 and 40% at k = 10.
- **Amazon e-mail example:** click-through revenue grows with send volume, so it rewards spamming. Charging an estimated lifetime loss per unsubscribe flipped many campaigns to negative and led to a per-campaign-family unsubscribe default.
- **Bing example:** a ranker bug raised queries and revenue in the short term. Short-term metrics can point opposite to long-term goals.
- Goodhart, Campbell, Lucas: historical correlations aren't causal levers.

### Ch 10 Complementary Techniques (`104`–`112`)

- Methods trade scale against depth: logs analysis, human evaluation, UER (tens of users), focus groups, surveys, external data.
- **Logs:** baselines, variance, funnel drop-offs, upper-bound sizing before building, natural experiments. Limitation: logs only show the past, and low usage may itself be caused by a poor design.
- **Focus groups and surveys:** stated preference often differs from behaviour. Surveys have wording, order and response biases, so trends are more useful than absolute levels.
- **External data:** use trends and direction, not absolute numbers. A smaller company can rely on published evidence for a general direction (e.g. that speed matters) and still test its own trade-offs.
- **Choosing a method:** qualitative early when ideas abound, quantitative later. **Triangulate** with several methods to bound the answer (the hierarchy of evidence).

### Ch 11 Observational Causal Studies (`113`–`121`)

- Causal effect = treated outcome − counterfactual. Randomization makes the selection-bias term zero in expectation.
- **When A/B is not possible:** the lever isn't yours, **too few units**, a control is too costly, the change is too expensive, the unit can't be randomized (TV → about 210 DMAs, low power), or it would be unethical. The recommendation is several lower-evidence methods together.
- **Interrupted time series:** model the pre-period and use the post-period gap as the effect. **Switching on/off repeatedly** reduces time confounds (seasonality, other changes) but risks users noticing inconsistency.
- **Interleaving:** for rankings only.
- **Regression discontinuity:** compare units just either side of a threshold. Invalid if units can manipulate their side or another policy shares the threshold. In software, a randomized test is often possible at the threshold instead.
- **IV / natural experiments:** lotteries, delivery-order quirks.
- **Propensity matching:** only handles observed confounders, so hidden bias remains.
- **Difference-in-differences:** treated change minus control change. It rests on parallel trends, and geo tests often use it.
- **Pitfalls:** unrecognized common causes (heavy users see more errors and churn less), spurious correlations, outliers, and assumptions you can't test.
- **Sidebar:** observational ad studies overstated lift by orders of magnitude compared with the matching experiment. The common cause was user activity.

### Ch 15 Ramping (`143`–`151`)

- The SQR framework balances speed, quality and risk across four phases. (1) **Pre-MPR**: rings (team → employees → beta → one data centre), automatic dial-up, near-real-time guardrails. (2) **MPR**, the maximum-power split, e.g. 50/50, for at least a week, longer if novelty or primacy shows. Little precision is gained after a week without trends. A 1-day run is biased toward heavy users. (3) **Post-MPR**: short operational ramps. (4) Optional **long-term holdout**, used only when useful (novelty/primacy risk, a very large short-term effect, an expected delayed effect, a long-term true-north metric). A holdout can be unethical if it withholds a known better experience. Keep the holdout at MPR when the effect is small.
- **Replicate surprising results.** The final iteration of a many-times-iterated test is biased upward.
- Ramp-down for a bad treatment should be fast. Clean up dead code after launch.
- Variance scales with 1/(q(1−q)), so a 50/50 split is the most sensitive.

### Ch 23 Measuring Long-Term Treatment Effects (`217`–`226`)

- Long term usually means 3+ months or N exposures. Short-term effects usually generalize, but not for price rises, low-quality content or ads, or "catchy" headlines.
- **Why effects drift:** user learning (both novelty and discoverability), network and supply constraints, delayed experience (travel, annual renewals), ecosystem change (seasonality, competitors, regulation, model drift, other launches, e.g. **notification fatigue as more teams send**).
- **Long-running experiments** suffer dilution, cookie churn, leakage, **survivorship** (which should also trigger SRM) and interaction with other launches. The gap between the first-week and last-week effect may be exogenous.
- **Alternatives:** cohort analysis (fixed stable-ID cohort, needs weighting to generalize); post-period analysis (turn off and measure the learned effect, but system-learned effects such as **opt-outs/unsubscribes** persist); time-staggered treatments (a convergence test with higher power); holdback (10% control after launch, low power); reverse experiment (move 10% back to control after launch, lets equilibrium form, may confuse users).

### Ch 17 Statistics (skimmed, `157`–`164`)

- Two-sample t-test; p-value vs CI duality.
- **Normality rule of thumb:** about 355·s² samples per variant, where s is the skewness. Capping revenue cuts the skewness and the required n. With small samples, use a **permutation test**.
- **Power:** 80% is standard, sized on the minimum effect worth detecting. Power is relative to the effect size. For small samples, consider **Type S (wrong sign) and Type M (exaggeration)** errors (Gelman & Carlin).
- **Multiple testing:** Bonferroni is simple but conservative. Benjamini–Hochberg is the alternative. Practical rule: tier metrics into expected, possible and unlikely to move, and use α of 0.05, 0.01 and 0.001.
- **Fisher's method** combines independent replications, which is useful when a single test is underpowered.

## 3. How ClimbOS should apply this (small, low-traffic businesses)

### 3a. Choosing an evaluation design (ClimbOS rule set, inspired by Ch 2, 10, 11, 15, 22)

1. **Is the outcome attributable and measurable inside the window?** If not, pick a surrogate driver metric and state that it is a proxy (Ch 7).
2. **Compute feasibility first:** eligible units per week, baseline rate, minimum worthwhile effect (MWE) → required n per arm → required weeks. If weeks ≤ the owner's horizon and units are independent, use a **user/visitor-level A/B** with a fixed horizon of at least 1 full week and whole weeks.
3. If n is short but **the business controls timing**, use a **switchback / time-based randomization** (random days or weeks on/off) or an **on/off ITS with reversals**. Not suitable when users notice inconsistency or effects carry over (e.g. price).
4. If there is a **comparable untreated location, segment or list**, use **difference-in-differences** with at least 4–8 pre-periods to check parallel trends.
5. If a **rule threshold** decides treatment (spend tier, lead score), consider **RDD**, or better, randomize near the threshold.
6. If an **exogenous event** happened (platform outage, competitor closure), treat it as a **natural experiment**, labelled observational.
7. If none of these apply, use the **qualitative/triangulation path** (UER/interviews, logs funnel, survey trend, external benchmarks). Label it "directional, not causal".
8. Always output the evidence-strength label: randomized > quasi-experimental with checks > before/after > anecdote/vendor case.

### 3b. Before/after with confounders

- Never report before/after delta as incremental.
- Required confounder checklist: seasonality and holidays, concurrent price/offer changes, other channel changes, external events, tracking changes, mix shift (new vs returning), and regression to the mean (a change made after a bad month).
- Prefer at least 8 pre-period points and a forecast counterfactual. Show the band, not a point estimate.
- If the owner can reverse the change cheaply, recommend an on/off schedule.

### 3c. Guardrails

- Every plan needs one goal metric, at most 4 drivers, and named guardrails: refunds/chargebacks, unsubscribe/spam-complaint rate, support tickets, margin, page speed, review rating, booking no-shows.
- Using indicator or capped variants gives lower variance.
- A violated guardrail blocks "ship" even when the goal metric improves (Ch 6, Ch 7 four-way rule).

### 3d. Reading platform-reported lifts (ads, e-mail tools, social)

- Platform attribution is observational. It double-counts users who would have converted anyway (Ch 11 sidebar).
- Platform metrics are often **not attributable to a variant** you control (Ch 7).
- Accept a platform "lift" as causal only if it comes from a randomized holdout or a geo/conversion-lift design whose control is disclosed. Otherwise label it "platform-reported, observational".
- Platform A/B tools: check SRM on delivered counts, check send-time parity (Ch 3 e-mail SRM), and check that the test ran whole weeks without early stopping.

### 3e. Novelty in e-mail/social tests

- Subject-line and creative gimmicks often decay. Compare week 1 with weeks 2+ and with the first-exposure cohort.
- Alternating patterns across sends beats a one-off.
- Measure downstream value and unsubscribes, not opens or clicks alone (Amazon OEC).
- Unsubscribes are a system-learned effect that persists after the test (Ch 23). Charge them as a lifetime cost.
- Frequency increases interact with other senders (notification fatigue, Ch 23).

## 4. Deterministic checks the app could implement

All are code, not model arithmetic. The formulas are standard statistics chosen by ClimbOS; the book's equation images were not extractable.

| Check | Rule |
|---|---|
| **SRM** | Chi-square goodness of fit of observed arm counts against the design ratio, df = arms−1. Use an exact binomial when an expected cell is below 5. p < 0.001 → `trust=fail`: hide the effect, block "ship", open a debugging checklist (assignment, logging, filters, send times, bots, restarts). 0.001–0.01 → warn. |
| **pp vs relative** | abs = pT − pC (label "percentage points"). rel = (pT − pC)/pC only if pC > 0 (label "%"). Always show the denominators. CI for rel via the delta method or Fieller, never var(Δ)/pC². |
| **MDE / sample size** | Per arm, n = (z₁₋α/₂·√(2p̄(1−p̄)) + z₁₋β·√(p₁(1−p₁)+p₂(1−p₂)))² / δ². Report weeks = n·arms / weekly eligible units, rounded up to whole weeks with a minimum of 1. Inverse: MDE given n. Flag when MDE > 2× MWE: "cannot detect what matters". Example: at a 3% baseline with 500/arm, MDE ≈ 3 pp absolute, i.e. a doubling. |
| **Multiple comparisons** | Show P(at least one false win) = 1 − (1−α)^k. Benjamini–Hochberg across declared secondary metrics and segments. Tiered α (0.05 / 0.01 / 0.001) by prior expectation. Unplanned segments are labelled exploratory. |
| **Peeking guard** | Fixed-horizon tests can't produce a verdict before the planned end date. Any interim view is labelled "monitoring only". Sequential methods are allowed only if chosen at plan lock. |
| **CI reading** | Classify each result into the Ch 2 six-case grid (sig/not sig × CI inside/outside MWE). "Not significant, CI includes MWE" → `inconclusive`, never `no effect`. |
| **Small-sample caution** | n per arm < 355·skew² or events < ~10 per arm → use a permutation or exact test and show the Type M exaggeration warning on "significant" results. |
| **Simpson guard** | If the allocation % changed during the run, compute per-period effects and an allocation-weighted total. Never pool raw counts. |
| **Segment migration guard** | Only allow segments defined from pre-exposure attributes for causal claims. |
| **Novelty check** | Effect by week, and effect for the first-exposure cohort. Flag a monotone decay greater than 50% from week 1. |
| **Duration check** | Whole-week multiples, ≥7 days, holiday overlap flagged. |
| **ITT check** | The analysis population is everyone assigned, not those who redeemed or opened. |
| **Unit check** | analysis unit finer than randomization unit → require delta method/bootstrap. Page-level randomization with user metrics → block. |
| **DiD check** | Pre-trend slope difference test on ≥4 pre-periods. Fail → downgrade to "descriptive". |

## 5. Proposed playbook contract (ClimbOS design, not the authors')

- **Required inputs:** decision and its cost/reversibility, goal metric, ≤4 drivers, guardrails with thresholds, MWE in business units, baseline and variance (or 8+ weeks of history), weekly eligible units, assignment mechanism available (randomizable? timing control? comparison group?), owner horizon, known concurrent changes and seasonality.
- **Eligibility:** A/B only if units are independent (no shared budget/market), required weeks ≤ horizon, assignment and logging verified. Otherwise route to the 3a ladder.
- **Steps:** feasibility → design choice → lock plan (metrics, α, horizon, method, segments) → instrument check / A/A where possible → run with guardrail monitoring and fast ramp-down → trust checks (SRM, duration, ITT, unit) → effect + CI + six-case class → guardrail and four-way decision → record → (if large or surprising) replicate or holdout.
- **Outputs:** design + evidence-strength label, plan record, trust report, effect table (abs pp, rel %, CI, n), decision (ship / stop / revise / investigate / inconclusive) with rationale, follow-up measurement (novelty re-check at +2–4 weeks).
- **Success measures** (for the engine): zero causal claims from failed-trust or observational-only runs, every result carries a CI and an evidence label, inconclusive rate is reported honestly, post-launch follow-up agrees with the test direction.
- **Stop conditions:** SRM p < 0.001 unresolved; guardrail breach beyond threshold (ramp down at once); instrumentation change mid-test; required duration > horizon with no feasible alternative design; request to change metrics or exclusions after seeing data.

## 6. Three evaluation case ideas

1. **Switchback for a cafe promo.** 400 weekly orders, wants to test a lunch bundle, same customers return daily. Expected: reject user-level A/B (contamination, low n), propose a randomized day-level schedule over ≥4 weeks with day-of-week balance, flag carry-over, and compute the MDE. Hard failure: a user-level split, or a verdict after 3 days.
2. **E-mail subject test with send-time imbalance and decay.** Variant A sent 9am, B sent 6pm; B has +18% opens in week 1 and +2% in week 3; unsubscribes are +0.4 pp for B. Expected: flag send-time confounding (Ch 3), flag novelty decay, put a lifetime cost on unsubscribes (Ch 7/23), and refuse to call B a winner on opens. Hard failure: "B wins, +18%".
3. **Two-branch DiD for a local ad campaign.** Ads in branch X only; X bookings +15%, branch Y +10% over the same weeks; 2 pre-periods available. Expected: DiD estimate ≈ +5 pp of relative growth (computed in code, with the units stated), parallel-trend check flagged as weak (only 2 pre-points), label "quasi-experimental, low confidence", suggest an on/off geo schedule. Hard failure: reporting +15% as campaign lift, or ignoring Y.
