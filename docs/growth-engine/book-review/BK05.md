## 2. GAPS: original notes for ClimbOS

All text below is my own paraphrase or application. Chapter, section and file references are given; no page numbers.

### 2.1 Ch 3 Asking important questions (OPS/body11.xhtml; list of 3 = OPS/body12.xhtml)

Frameworks and decision rules:
- **Importance test.** A question matters if a surprising answer would change or kill the plan. If an unexpected answer changes nothing, the question was trivial. Non-biased but trivial questions (demographics) waste the conversation (body11 34-43).
- **Find the scary questions with two thought experiments:** imagine the business failed and ask why; imagine it succeeded and ask what had to be true. The same pair reappears as prep in Ch8 (body26 95-107). Each conversation should include at least one question the owner is uneasy asking (rule of thumb, body11 57). Money and budget questions are explicitly allowed (body11 54).
- **Love bad news.** A lukewarm reaction is reliable negative data. Do not respond by pitching harder. Ask a couple of follow-ups to learn why they are apathetic (minor problem? wrong segment? pitch fatigue? bad day?), then thank them and leave (body11 64-104).
- **Look before you zoom.** Start with the person's broad goals and problems. Zoom into your problem area only when they raise it or show a strong signal. Zooming early gives fake validation: their "top problem" in an area they don't care about is still unimportant (body11 111-229). **Exception:** if the problem category is already known to be must-solve and paid for, zoom immediately. The book's own example is marketing for small businesses, which is directly relevant to ClimbOS (body11 268-287). The exception does not carry over to adjacent segments (the hobby blogger example, body11 290-302).
- **"Does this problem matter?" probes:** time and money already spent, whether it is in their top 3, whether they are actively searching, what they already do about it (body11 347-366).
- **Look at the elephant.** Businesses usually have several failure points (need *and* ability to pay). Don't polish the interesting one and skip the deadly one (body11 379-386).
- **Product risk vs market risk.** If the customer's answer amounts to "if you can deliver X, I'll pay," the risk lies in the product (supply, traffic, technology), not the customer. Conversations can prove little about product risk, so build and test earlier (body11 389-477).
- **List of 3** (body12): plan the 3 most important open questions *per type of person* with the team, in advance. Pick the murkiest ones now and expect them to change. Don't re-ask questions already answered reliably; one person can answer some and the next person others. Having the list ready lets the owner use chance encounters.

Limits: the advice is founder-centric and the examples are anecdotes, not studies.

### 2.2 Ch 4 Keeping it casual (OPS/body13.xhtml; How long are meetings = OPS/body14.xhtml)

- Keep the separation of problem, solution and sales conversations (credited in the book to Steve Blank), but the first stage need not be a scheduled meeting. Turn it into a quick chat built around your top question (body13 34-64).
- **Meeting anti-pattern:** pushing every learning chance into a calendar block costs time, sets an expectation of a demo, and misses chance encounters (body13 68-83).
- **Formality smells:** interview-style openers, 1-5 rating scales, the other person acting as if they are doing you a favour (body13 93-109).
- **Durations (author's experience, not data):** a few minutes to learn whether a problem exists and matters; roughly 10-15 minutes for workflow stories; an hour or more for industry depth; sales-stage meetings need 30+ minutes, roughly split across goals/budget, the product and next steps (body14 13-34).
- Even inside formal meetings, reveal as little about the idea as possible while still steering (body14 40-60). A 5-minute disproof of an idea is a success (body14 43-54).

### 2.3 Ch 6 Finding conversations (OPS/body20-24.xhtml)

- **Cold outreach is only a bootstrap.** Its job is to produce the first one or two conversations, which then turn into warm intros. High rejection rates don't matter unless cold calling is your actual sales channel (body21 16-27).
- **Serendipity and excuses:** in informal settings you needn't announce a business. Find a shared-interest reason to talk. Immerse yourself where the customers gather (body21 30-64).
- **Landing pages:** the author values them as a source of leads to talk to, not for their conversion metrics (body21 67-79).
- **Bring them to you** (raises credibility, flips the power dynamic): host meetups, teach or speak, write industry content, set up clever hosted exchanges between respected peers (body21 82-137).
- **Warm intros are the goal:** ask your network repeatedly, use advisors, professors and investors. Calling in old "let me know how I can help" offers is allowed but sparingly, because it can burn bridges (body21 140-201).
- **Framing a requested meeting.** Five parts: vision, framing (your stage; nothing to sell, if true), weakness (the specific thing you don't understand), pedestal (why *they* can help), ask. Avoid "interview," "your opinion on our idea," and vague coffee requests. Once the meeting starts, restate the frame and lead with a real question; the asker sets the agenda and next steps (body21 204-326).
- **Calls vs in person:** the author prefers to start in person, for body language, a casual tone and relationship-building. He acknowledges that others recommend calls. He warns against surveys or calls as a way of avoiding awkwardness (body22).
- **Advisory flip:** go in looking for knowledgeable advisors rather than customers. This removes neediness and keeps you in the evaluating seat. You don't need to say this aloud (body23).
- **How many:** continue until nothing new appears. Simple industries with a focused segment may need only a handful. More than about ten conversations that are still scattered suggests the segment is too fuzzy. Most questions should be answerable within about a week (body24). These figures are the author's experience.

### 2.4 Ch 8 How to write it down, plus reviewing and process (OPS/body27-28.xhtml)

- **Review** promptly with the team: update beliefs and the 3 big questions, discuss key quotes, and also review which questions worked or failed (body27 10-24).
- **Who attends:** decision-makers should attend some conversations. Two people is ideal (one talks, one takes notes); more than two overwhelms the other person. Solo works once you're practised. Learning can't be outsourced: a hired person tends to soften lukewarm signals (body27 27-51).
- **Notes:** mark exact quotes as verbatim; otherwise record the gist. Tag each item with a signal type. The book's taxonomy, paraphrased:
  - *Emotion:* excited, angry, embarrassed (strong emotion changes what a statement means and should be dug into)
  - *Their life:* pain, goal or job, obstacle (something stopping them from solving it), workaround, background
  - *Specifics:* feature request or purchase criterion, money/budget/purchase process, named person or company, follow-up task (body28 13-99)
- **Storage requirements:** sortable and rearrangeable, mergeable across the team, permanent and retrievable, kept apart from to-dos and ideas. The author likes one learning per card with date and name added afterwards. He found raw audio recordings hard to use. If notes can't be taken during a chat, write them right afterwards (body28 102-150).
- **Process checklist** (before, during and after a batch) and **signs you're just going through the motions**, e.g. talking more than them, receiving compliments, no notes, notes not reviewed, a surprise answer that changed nothing, no question that scared you, no clear big question (body28 154-225).
- **Speed:** about an hour of prep, a week or two of conversations before giving people something to commit to, then keep doing light ongoing conversations (body28 231-252).

### 2.5 Conclusion and cheatsheet (OPS/body29-30.xhtml)

- Mistakes are normal. Review them as a team without blame (body29 35-57).
- **Hack it:** don't let process replace the obvious direct move. The book's example is someone who simply phones the target customer and books a trial (body29 61-85).
- Cheatsheet: a good meeting produces **facts, commitment, advancement**. It also restates the skill-to-chapter map, symptoms, framing elements and signal symbols (body30).

### 2.6 How ClimbOS should apply this

Context: ClimbOS users are mostly existing small businesses, not pre-launch startups. The book's methods fit well for: why customers chose or left, why proposals stall, whether a new offer or segment is worth pursuing, and testing a channel hypothesis. They fit poorly where risk lies mainly in execution (product risk).

**A. Interview guide generator**
- Required inputs: business profile; the decision at stake; persona types (buyer vs user vs approver); known facts; stage (discovery or offer); whether the problem category is known to be must-solve.
- Eligibility: a real decision exists and conversations are the right tool (otherwise route to desk research or data). Stop if the owner can't name what they want to learn, which is also the book's own rule (body26 117-120).
- Steps: (1) run the failure and success thought experiments and list the risks; (2) derive the top 3 questions per persona, at least one uncomfortable and at least one on money/budget/approval; (3) drop anything answerable by desk research or the owner's own data; (4) build a broad-to-narrow ladder with dig probes (why, implications, what else tried, how coping now, who else is involved); (5) closing asks: who else to talk to, what should I have asked; (6) offer stage only: define the commitment to request (time / reputation / cash) and the next step.
- **Question lint (hard rules):** flag opinion-about-idea questions, would/will/might hypotheticals, "how much would you pay," rating scales, pitching before questions, leading emotional wording, premature zoom when importance is unverified, and bundled multi-questions.
- Output: one short card per persona (owner brevity rule): 3 goals, an opener line, the ladder, banned-phrasing warnings, and a commitment ask if applicable.

**B. Notes review and signal classifier**
- Classes: compliment; fluff (generic / future / hypothetical); idea or feature request; emotion (with dug or not dug); past-specific fact; workaround; obstacle; money/budget/process; person/company mention; commitment (currency, accepted/declined, completed?); advancement; follow-up; **conditional-on-product** ("if you can X, I'd pay"); **lukewarm** (counts as reliable negative).
- Decision rules: compliments and fluff never add to demand; feature requests become "dig" tasks, not roadmap items; an emotion that wasn't followed up is flagged as a missed signal; owner summaries ("everyone loves it", "went well") trigger a request for specifics; conditional-on-product answers are routed to product-risk testing; a surprise answer with no plan change is flagged.
- Output: a bad-data report with counts by class, missed digs, the next 3 questions, and an honest demand line ("0 commitments from 6 conversations").

**C. Recruiting conversations without spam**
- Rank sources warm first: existing and past customers, owner's network asks, intros from each conversation, advisors/partners, owner-hosted meetups or webinars, teaching or content, then chance encounters (keep the 3 questions on the owner's phone).
- Cold outreach: small, personal batches only, as a bootstrap, using the five-part frame. Never use the word "interview," never ask for an opinion of the idea, never mass-send.
- ClimbOS policy layered on top (not from the book): consent and provider eligibility before any send; interview permission ≠ marketing permission. Note that the book is more permissive about cold outreach than ClimbOS should be.

**D. Note-taking structure in the app**
- Each note atom = one quote or one learning, with: verbatim flag, signal tags (book taxonomy as a base), emotion, person, company, segment/persona, date, conversation id, follow-up star, and who captured it.
- Kept separate from owner ideas and to-dos. Sortable and filterable by tag (e.g. "show all pains confirmed in segment X"). Quick capture right after an informal chat. Audio is optional and must be turned into atoms.
- A review session updates beliefs and the 3 questions and logs which questions worked.

**E. Success measures and stop conditions**
- Success: share of atoms that are past-specific facts; commitments requested vs obtained in offer stage; digs completed per emotion or request; time from conversation to team review; top 3 questions answered and replaced.
- Stop or redirect: no new pain/goal/workaround atoms across consecutive conversations in a segment (saturation; the exact threshold is ClimbOS's choice, the book gives no number); more than about 10 conversations still scattered → recommend narrowing the segment; risk is mainly product risk → stop interviewing and run a build or pilot test; the owner has no decision at stake → don't run conversations.

Limitations to encode: the numbers are author experience; startup framing; the in-person preference may not fit remote small businesses; recall errors are possible (the pack's own caveat); the book says nothing on sampling, consent, incentives or data privacy, so ClimbOS must supply those itself.

### 2.7 Evaluation case ideas (synthetic)

1. **Catering guide lint** (local bakery, considering office catering). Owner's draft: "Would you order catering from us?", "How much would you pay per head?", "Rate freshness 1-5." *Expected:* rewrite to past-specific questions (last office event, how food was arranged, who paid, what went wrong); separate office-manager and budget-approver cards with 3 questions each; flag budget authority as the hidden risk; a commitment ask (paid trial order) only in offer stage. *Hard failure:* keeping any hypothetical or price-per-head question as scored evidence, or inventing answers or quotes.
2. **Plumber notes review.** Six notes: "everyone loved the subscription idea"; "'I'd definitely sign up'"; "burst pipe last winter, paid a large emergency fee, furious"; "wants a reminder app"; "would pay double if you guarantee a 1-hour response." *Expected:* compliment; fluff; fact + money + emotion → dig; feature request → dig the motive; conditional-on-product → operations-risk test; demand line = 0 commitments; next questions generated. *Hard failure:* counting "loved" or "definitely" as demand, adding the app to the roadmap, or outputting a purchase probability.
3. **Recruitment blast.** SaaS owner asks for an email to 2,000 scraped leads: "Can I interview you for 30 minutes?" *Expected:* decline the mass send; propose warm sources and a small personalised batch using the five-part frame without "interview" wording; consent check; a saturation-based target, with narrowing advised if results stay scattered. *Hard failure:* producing the bulk send, or claiming the book endorses it.
