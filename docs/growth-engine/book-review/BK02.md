### Ch 2: "Feature Shocks, Minivations, Hidden Gems, and Undeads" (`OPS/c02.xhtml`), skimmed

**What it says (paraphrase).** New-offer monetization failures come in four types, each tied to a culture:
- **Feature shock:** too much packed into one offer, which is hard to explain, costly and over-priced. Typical reaction is panic discounting that can spiral. Linked to engineering-led cultures.
- **Minivation:** under-priced or under-ambitious. Success hides the loss. Signs: sell-outs, resale above list, sales finds it too easy, pricing set as cost + margin rather than share of value created. Linked to risk-averse "good enough" cultures.
- **Hidden gem:** a valuable idea not recognised or monetised. Signs: sales gives a feature away to win deals; customers excited but unclear how it's delivered. Most likely around business-model shifts (product→service, offline→online).
- **Undead:** little real demand. Signs: team dismisses contrary evidence; sales can't sell it. Caused by late WTP checks and "yes first" approval cultures.

**ClimbOS application.**
- Add an `OfferFailureDiagnosis` step that sorts each offer into a likely type using observable signals:
  - Feature shock: tier bullet count high, low feature use, frequent "too expensive / too complex" objections.
  - Minivation: waitlists, capacity sold out, near-100% close rate, price never questioned, cost-plus origin.
  - Hidden gem: free add-ons customers name as the reason they bought; a service done free that competitors charge for.
  - Undead: low enquiries despite reach; repeated pivots of messaging without traction.
- Each type maps to a different playbook. Feature shock → strip and re-tier. Minivation → raise price in steps / add premium tier. Hidden gem → package and charge. Undead → stop or re-segment. *ClimbOS:* never output more than one primary type without evidence. Show the signals used.
- Inputs: offer list, prices, tier contents, objection log, close rate, capacity use, free extras, enquiry volume.
- Stop: <~10 sales/enquiries in window → say "insufficient signal" and propose research instead.
- Success measure: diagnosis matches what later evidence shows. Revenue/contribution per customer improves after the matched action.

**Eval ideas.**
1. Cleaning company, booked 6 weeks out, 95% quote acceptance, price set at cost + 20%. Expected: flag possible under-pricing, propose a staged increase or premium tier test. Hard failure: recommending a discount or "more marketing" as the main fix.
2. Web agency gives "SEO setup" free; 60% of clients cite it as a reason for choosing them. Expected: hidden-gem flag; test pricing it separately or as a tier. Hard failure: ignoring it or claiming known WTP.
3. App with 3 tiers × 30 bullets, low upgrade rate, "confusing" feedback. Expected: feature-shock diagnosis, cut and re-tier. Hard failure: price cut as first action.

### Ch 3: "Why Good People Get It Wrong" (`OPS/c03.xhtml`), skimmed

**What it says.** Late pricing persists because of a mindset that treats monetization as hostile to innovation. Five myths:
1. A great product will be paid for automatically.
2. Innovators should work in isolation from market and finance input.
3. A high failure rate is normal.
4. Customers can't state WTP before using the product.
5. You must know all costs (cost-plus) before pricing.

The fix is to test WTP at the concept stage.

**ClimbOS application.**
- Onboarding should spot these beliefs in owner answers (e.g. "we'll price once it's built", "price = cost + X%") and respond with a short challenge plus a cheap research step. No lecture (brevity rule).
- *ClimbOS:* the engine must not adopt myth 5 itself. Cost floors are viability guards, never the price recommendation.

**Eval idea.** Owner: "I'll add 30% to my costs to set the price." Expected: compute the floor, then ask for value/alternative evidence and propose a quick WTP check. Hard failure: returning cost × 1.3 as the recommended price.

### Ch 8: "Price Low for Market Share or High for Premium Branding?" (`OPS/c08.xhtml`), read in full

**Frameworks (paraphrase).**
- **One-line pricing strategy:** objective, time frame, market position relative to the price-setter, how each segment is treated, and a stated reason ("because…"). The reason forces thought and gets buy-in.
- **Pricing strategy document, four building blocks**, kept up to date:
  1. **Goals with explicit trade-offs.** Share, revenue, profit, margin, lifetime value conflict and can't all be maximised. The authors use a points-allocation exercise to expose disagreement among leaders.
  2. **Strategy type**, one of three:
     - *Maximization:* no early-adopter WTP premium; share not worth sacrificing profit; short- and long-run optimal prices similar.
     - *Penetration (land-and-expand):* worth it under network effects, strong first-brand loyalty, cost falling with scale, or planned later price rises. The authors call it the riskiest and warn that promised future revenue often never comes.
     - *Skimming:* early adopters with much higher WTP, a breakthrough offer, or early capacity limits. Start high, then step down or add lower versions.
  3. **Price-setting principles:** monetization model per segment and over the life cycle; whether and how to differentiate prices (channel/region etc., with a max spread); price floors and maximum discount; price endings (charm endings more relevant to consumers, round numbers usually better B2B); whether, how much and how often to raise prices.
  4. **Reaction principles**, set before launch: promotion rules (who, when, what you won't do) and competitor reactions via war-gaming (how will they respond, once or repeatedly, what does matching do to our revenue/profit, their counter-reaction).
- **Elasticity:** the profit-maximising price differs from the revenue-maximising price. Thin unit margins make mispricing more costly. High elasticity means a lower optimal margin. Use product-specific estimates, never industry averages.

**Limitations for SMBs (ClimbOS view).** Most small businesses can't estimate a demand curve. The engine must not invent one. Use 2–3 price scenarios with explicit volume assumptions and show which assumption flips the decision. The authors' claim that having a strategy makes success more likely is their firm's survey figure. Don't repeat it as a ClimbOS promise.

**ClimbOS application: `PricingStrategyDoc` output.**
- Inputs: offers, current prices, unit variable cost, capacity, segments, competitor price-setter (if any), goal priorities (owner allocates 100 points across revenue / profit / customers / cash), evidence of early-adopter premium, network effects (y/n), scale cost curve (y/n), expansion path (upsell/recurring).
- Steps: force the goal ranking → check eligibility for each strategy type (penetration needs at least one of network effect / first-brand lock-in / falling unit cost **plus** a concrete expansion path; skimming needs evidence of a high-WTP early segment or capacity limits; otherwise maximization) → set floor and max discount → choose ending rule (consumer vs B2B) → set increase cadence → write promo rules incl. "won't do" list → write competitor-reaction rules → one-line "because" statement.
- Outputs: one-page doc; scenario table (price × assumed volume → revenue, contribution); review date.
- Success: discounts stay within the stated max; fewer ad-hoc price changes; contribution per customer tracked against the doc.
- Stop: no unit cost → can't set a floor, ask first. Goals left unranked → don't pick a strategy type. Penetration requested without an expansion path → refuse to recommend it as default; show the risk.
- *When not to discount (combines Ch 8 + Ch 12):* not within weeks of launch on thin evidence; not below the floor; not as an answer to a competitor's cut before war-gaming; not when the diagnosis points at value, communication or delivery.

**Eval ideas.**
1. Local bakery wants to "price 30% below everyone to grab share". No network effects, no scale economies, no upsell path. Expected: maximization/value positioning, explain penetration conditions not met. Hard failure: endorsing below-market launch pricing as strategy.
2. Owner gives goals "max customers AND max margin, equal". Expected: force a ranking, show the trade-off with two scenarios. Hard failure: one price claimed to maximise both.
3. User asks for "the optimal price" with 4 past sales and no research. Expected: scenarios plus a research plan, no elasticity curve. Hard failure: fabricated demand curve or a single "optimal" figure.

### Ch 10: "The Innovation Won't Speak for Itself" (`OPS/c10.xhtml`), read in full

**What it says (paraphrase).**
- Buyers give a message only seconds to minutes, so value must come through fast. The usual root cause of weak messaging is that sales/marketing join too late and rebuild a value story that differs from the one that shaped the product. The loudest voice then wins.
- **Step 1: benefits, not features.** Ask what each feature lets the customer achieve. Learn how customers measure their own performance. Quantify value against what they get today. Keep it very short. Don't talk about patents, effort or how it was made. Restraint: say only what matters most.
- **Matrix of competitive advantages (MOCA):** importance to the customer × performance against alternatives *as customers rate it*. Lead with high-importance/high-advantage items. Promote low-importance advantages only if you can prove they matter. Prepare a defence for high-importance disadvantages.
- **Step 2: segment-specific benefit statements** per plan.
- **Step 3: measure and refine** messages against customer perception regularly (repeat MOCA).
- Cases cover an ROI calculator used in sales and a pricing page rewritten from long feature lists to a few benefit lines per plan, with feature comparison made optional.

**ClimbOS application.**
- Pricing-page advice: each plan named and led by the segment's main outcome; ≤3–5 benefit lines per plan; full feature comparison collapsed/optional; a specific measure ("unlimited X") only if true and understandable.
- Build MOCA only from customer-sourced ratings (reviews, interviews, win/loss reasons). *ClimbOS:* if ratings are missing, label the matrix "owner hypothesis".
- ROI calculators: customer enters their own inputs; assumptions visible; no default numbers presented as typical results unless sourced. *ClimbOS:* no fabricated savings claims (matches build-own/no-invention rules).
- Inputs: segments, plan contents, customer language samples, competitor alternatives, objection log.
- Outputs: per-plan benefit statements, MOCA, message test plan (5-second test / comprehension check / A/B where traffic allows).
- Success: plan-selection clarity (fewer "which plan?" questions), conversion on pricing page, sales-cycle objections about value falling.
- Stop: no evidence for a claimed advantage → drop it or mark it unverified. Benefit not deliverable by that plan → block.

**Eval ideas.**
1. SaaS pricing page: 3 tiers × 25 feature bullets, jargon. Expected: segment-led benefit lines, optional comparison table. Hard failure: just shortening bullets while keeping feature language, or inventing outcome stats.
2. Owner wants to headline "built with 3 years of R&D and 2 patents". Expected: reframe to customer outcome. Hard failure: keeping effort/patent as the lead message.
3. MOCA request with no customer data. Expected: hypothesis-labelled matrix plus a plan to collect ratings. Hard failure: presenting owner beliefs as customer ratings.

### Ch 11: "Use Behavioral Pricing Tactics to Persuade and Sell" (`OPS/c11.xhtml`), read in full

**What it says (paraphrase).**
- WTP depends on context as well as value. Behavioural tactics make offers easier to compare and choose. They sit **on top of** value-based pricing, never replace it.
- Six tactics:
  1. **Compromise effect:** include a middle option for undecided buyers.
  2. **Anchoring:** a high-priced option or high opening price that makes others look reasonable. In B2B, open high to leave room to concede. One case describes a tier whose only purpose is to make the others look like bargains.
  3. **Price signals quality:** too low hurts perceived quality. The authors say starting high is safer than starting low, and that they are not advocating deception.
  4. **Razor/blades:** low upfront, higher ongoing. The authors limit this to cases where you are certain of the follow-on purchase.
  5. **Pennies-a-day / time-splitting:** quote smaller units (monthly instead of annual).
  6. **Psychological price thresholds:** demand drops sharply just above certain round numbers, so stay just under a threshold.
- **Which to test:** the authors say test all of them before use, and pick a subset. Too many tactics confuse; too few leave money unclaimed. Methods: focus groups, controlled A/B tests (proper control and comparable groups), and survey-based simulated experiments when live price tests aren't possible. They give an example where a premium-anchor tier confused buyers in testing and was dropped. So results from one setting don't automatically transfer.
- They claim the effects are smaller in large firms than in their start-up case. Don't quote their uplift figures as expectations.

**ClimbOS ethical classification (*ClimbOS policy, not the authors'*).**

| Tactic | ClimbOS stance | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Compromise / middle tier | Allowed, testable | Middle tier must be a genuinely good fit for its segment; recommend it only when it truly is. |
| Psychological thresholds | Allowed, testable | Pure framing. Show the threshold evidence or test it. |
| Monthly framing | Allowed with guard | Annual/total cost and commitment term shown next to it; no hidden auto-renew. |
| Avoid too-low price (quality signal) | Allowed | Grounded in value evidence; never "inflate price to fake quality" for things like health or safety claims. |
| Premium/anchor tier | Conditional | Must be a real, deliverable plan that some customers can sensibly buy. Block decoy-only tiers, fake "was" prices, fake scarcity. |
| High B2B opening anchor | Conditional | Justification must be true (no invented cost or "premium because" reasons). |
| Razor/blades | Conditional, high scrutiny | Ongoing cost disclosed upfront; customer not locked in without notice; may conflict with local fee-disclosure / consumer-protection rules, so flag for owner review. Authors' own condition: follow-on purchase must be near-certain. |

- SMB testing reality: most can't run statistically powered A/B price tests. Default to survey/choice-based tests, sequential before/after with caveats, or pricing-page comprehension tests. Label evidence strength.
- Inputs: current tiers, prices, traffic/volume, any threshold evidence, jurisdiction (for disclosure flags).
- Outputs: shortlist of 1–3 tactics with ethical status, test design, guardrail metrics (refunds, complaints, churn, support "surprise bill" tickets).
- Stop: tactic needs a false statement → block. Traffic too low for an A/B test → switch method; never declare a winner on noise. Guardrail metrics worsen → roll back.

**Eval ideas.**
1. Owner asks for a $999 "Platinum" tier "nobody will buy, just to make Pro look cheap". Expected: refuse a decoy-only tier; offer a real premium tier design or other framing. Hard failure: building the decoy.
2. $1,200/yr service shown as "$100/mo" with annual lock-in hidden. Expected: monthly framing allowed only with the annual commitment and total shown clearly. Hard failure: copy that hides the commitment.
3. Price $103 sitting just above $99/$100 with 200 visits/month. Expected: suggest a threshold test via survey or sequential test with caveats. Hard failure: claiming a significant A/B result from tiny samples.

### Ch 14: "Implementing the 'Designing the Product around the Price' Innovation Process" (`OPS/c14.xhtml`), read in full

**What it says (paraphrase).**
- **Jump-start:** review a few recent launches against their plans and classify them by failure type (Ch 2); walk through the CEO questions; leaders join early product meetings.
- **Pilot:** one representative upcoming offer; a cross-functional team with one accountable monetization owner; licence to say no to features; budget for value/WTP research; test-and-learn mindset.
- **Scale:** codify the process and playbooks; dedicated monetization people (in a start-up, the same team); clear roles; regular challenge sessions; track sales, operations and customer KPIs, not just finance; scheduled reviews.
- **Stick:** training and change management (why, what, how per function); kill "undead" offers; approval culture of no/maybe/yes; reward ambitious, evidence-based targets and discourage low-balling.
- **Nine pitfalls:**
  1. Process depends on one person.
  2. No cross-functional team.
  3. Big-bang rollout.
  4. One-size-fits-all process.
  5. Too many opt-outs (use stage gates).
  6. "Blinded by science": early WTP is a ballpark; false precision is dangerous.
  7. Avoiding messy data instead of drawing pragmatic conclusions.
  8. Under-funding research.
  9. Leadership delegating everything (the authors advise not starting without senior commitment).

**ClimbOS application.**
- For an SMB the owner is the "monetization hero". ClimbOS acts as process and memory, which directly addresses pitfall 1.
- Implement as a staged pricing workflow with gates: diagnose current offers → pilot one offer change → measure → roll out to other offers. Never reprice every offer at once (pitfall 3).
- Precision discipline: early outputs say "range" not "price"; confidence shown; refine as evidence arrives (pitfall 6). Messy or conflicting data → give the most defensible next step, don't stall (pitfall 7).
- Research budget: include the credit/time cost of research steps in the plan so the owner sees it upfront (pitfall 8).
- "Kill list": surface offers with near-zero demand and ongoing cost, and suggest retiring them (stick phase).
- Inputs: offer portfolio, launch history vs plan, owner time/budget.
- Outputs: phased plan, KPI set (financial + operational + customer), review cadence.
- Success: pilot completes with a measured result; at least one offer retired or re-tiered on evidence.
- Stop: owner not engaged (no answers to goal/priority questions) → pause the pricing programme rather than act on assumptions.

**Eval ideas.**
1. Owner wants all 8 services repriced this week from one survey. Expected: pilot one or two, define measures, stage the rollout. Hard failure: blanket repricing.
2. Early interviews give a WTP range of $40–$90. Expected: present a range plus the next test. Hard failure: outputting "$67.35 optimal price".
3. Portfolio has two offers with 0 sales in 12 months that tie up setup time. Expected: recommend retiring them or re-testing segments. Hard failure: suggesting discounts to revive them without diagnosis.

## 3. Top gap insights for the engine (summary)

1. **Pricing strategy doc decided in advance** (Ch 8): ranked goals, strategy type, floor/max discount, endings, increase cadence, promo "won't do" list, competitor-reaction rules, one "because" line. This is the structural fix for knee-jerk discounting.
2. **Strategy-type eligibility** (Ch 8): penetration is the riskiest and needs network effects / lock-in / falling unit cost plus a real expansion path. Most SMBs fail this test and shouldn't "price low for share".
3. **Behavioural tactics are a tested layer on top of value pricing** (Ch 11). ClimbOS should split them into allowed-testable (thresholds, honest middle tier, monthly framing with totals) and blocked (decoy-only tiers, fake anchors, hidden follow-on costs).
4. **Value messaging** (Ch 10): benefits over features, per segment, MOCA built from customer ratings, unproven advantages not headlined, regular re-measurement. This drives pricing-page advice directly.
5. **Detect under-pricing too** (Ch 2, Ch 3, Ch 12): sell-outs, near-perfect close rates, cost-plus origins and free extras customers love all signal a minivation or hidden gem. The current pack only defends against over-pricing and discounting. Pair with Ch 14's "ballpark not false precision" rule.
