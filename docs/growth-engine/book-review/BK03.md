## Part 2: Gaps (original ClimbOS notes, paraphrased)

### Ch 1, Positioning as Context (chapter1.xhtml)

**Core ideas**
- Buyers build context from every cue they see: messaging, pricing, features, brand, partners and existing customers. A strong product in the wrong context goes unrecognised. Her example is a world-class performer who goes unnoticed outside a station (ch1 49–102).
- Choosing a category quietly decides a lot: target buyers, where you sell, competitors, price and margin, and the roadmap. Her example is the same batter sold as cake versus muffins (159–227).
- Two "default positioning" traps:
  1. The product has drifted away from what the founders meant to build, and they haven't noticed (155–245).
  2. The market around the product has moved on (247–277).
- Deliberate positioning means choosing the frame where the distinctive features look natural rather than odd. Her example: a stick on a cake is weird, but on a lollipop it belongs (294–338).
- Customers can reframe a product better than its founders: her database was reframed as a data warehouse (354–392).

**Engine rules**
- Run a default-frame audit. Compare the owner's self-description, which often reflects the founding idea, with how customers describe the product in reviews, testimonials and support tickets.
- Treat a mismatch as a signal to reposition, not as proof.
- Flag a category whose standard expected features the product lacks. That is the email-without-a-calendar pattern (ch2 385–397).

**Limitation**
- The framing is mostly B2B tech. Many small local services are fixed to a search category ("plumber"), so the real choice there is usually the subsegment, not the category.

### Ch 2, Five (plus One) Components (chapter2.xhtml)

**Core ideas**
- She rejects fill-in-the-blank positioning statements:
  - they assume you already know the answers
  - they reinforce the status quo, which favours incumbents
  - they never lead to action
  - they're hard to remember (164–195)
- The components:
  1. Competitive alternatives: what customers would do without you
  2. Unique attributes: what you have that the alternatives lack
  3. Value and proof
  4. Target market characteristics
  5. Market category
  6. Optional: relevant trend (202–239)
- Proof standard: the owner's opinion doesn't count. Customer, reviewer and expert opinions and data do. Purely qualitative value claims won't be believed (306–310).
- Firmographics alone rarely identify fit. Best-fit buyers buy fast, rarely ask for discounts, and refer others (326–330).
- A category triggers assumptions about competitors, features and price. If you choose well, those assumptions do your selling. If you choose badly, you spend your effort fighting them (353–378).
- A trend is not a category. "AI" tells buyers nothing about what a product is; "AI plus a category" does (446–459).
- Order matters: alternatives → attributes → value → who cares → category → trend. Starting from features or from segments produces positioning that doesn't connect (498–507). The flow diagram after 508 is an image and was not extractable.

**Engine rules**
- Never produce a positioning statement as the primary output.
- Enforce the dependency order. Each component must reference the one before it.
- Reject any trend-only headline that names no category.

### Step 2, Form a Positioning Team (chapter4.xhtml)

**Core ideas**
- Positioning is business strategy. The business owner (CEO or founder) must drive it, and marketing can't "own" it (26–39).
- It feeds marketing messaging and targeting, sales segmentation, customer-success onboarding, and the product roadmap (41–63).
- Include one or two senior people per function, three to twelve in total, because each function sees customers at different points (66–86).
- She recommends an outside facilitator who challenges assumptions people treat as untouchable (90–94).

**ClimbOS application**
- For a solo owner, the "team" is the owner plus evidence from each customer touchpoint: sales conversations, support threads, reviews and delivery notes.
- The engine can act as the outside facilitator: it surfaces conflicting assumptions and challenges the default.
- Final selection still requires explicit owner sign-off, because this is a strategy decision. No positioning version should go live without the owner's approval.

### Step 3, Vocabulary and Baggage (chapter5.xhtml)

**Core ideas**
- Agree shared definitions first: what positioning is, its components, and how market maturity and competition affect which style fits (24–37).
- "Baggage" is the product's history built into its current positioning: the original market and the original terms. Customers carry none of it (82–94).
- The key output of this step is agreement that the original market may no longer be the best one, and that opinions are suspended for the exercise (96–99).
- Examples: the baking-soda-to-deodoriser and industrial-robot-to-self-driving-vehicle repositionings (56–80, 106–137). These are anecdotes, not controlled evidence.

**ClimbOS application**
- Add a baggage scan. Record the current default frame and the founding terms explicitly. Mark which site phrases come from the origin story rather than from customer language.
- Get an owner acknowledgment that alternatives will be considered.
- Score the default frame alongside the alternatives. Don't silently preserve it.

### Step 8, Market Frame of Reference (chapter10.xhtml)

**Ground rule**
- A "market" must already exist in buyers' minds. The only exception is a deliberate decision to create one.
- The frame exists to trigger helpful assumptions about competitors, features and price (30–36).

**Ways to find candidate frames**
1. **Abductive reasoning** (44–49): take the key features and value, and ask what kind of product usually has them.
2. **Adjacent markets, especially growing ones** (53–65): borders between markets blur, and growth brings attention. Only choose one if it makes your strengths obvious; wanting to belong isn't enough.
3. **Ask customers, carefully** (69–84): customers place you in markets tied to their own job or industry. Past confusion skews their answers. They usually pick the most obvious category, which is often not the best one.

**Three positioning styles** (155–191)

| Style | When it fits | Work required | Risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Head to Head: win an existing market | You are already the leader. Or the category is understood but has no clear leader yet (234–243). Rarely right for a small or new business (226–228). Large challengers only when something shifts against the leader (230–232). | A leader defends the current buying criteria. A challenger needs hard evidence of superiority on those criteria (265–271). | You must match buyers' existing definition (280–284). An emerging leaderless category fills up fast and may need funding; poor fit for bootstrappers (253–261). |
| Big Fish, Small Pond: win a subsegment | The category is well defined and has a clear leader that isn't you (376–379). An identifiable subsegment exists, meaning you could actually build a list of it (381–386). Its unmet need is specific and important (388–394). You meet it much better than the leader (396–400). | Teach the subsegment that the generic solution fails them. Show proof of the gap and ideally quantify the value. Prove you are "good enough" on the core category criteria (405–411). | The leader ships a good-enough feature or buys a competitor. Prefer advantages the leader would be harmed by copying (415–419). The segment may be too small; check sales needed against segment size (360–365). |
| Create a New Game: create a category | Only after ruling out every existing category, or if you are large enough to command attention (551–552). Needs a major shift in technology, economics, regulation or behaviour (556–562). Also consider it when you would otherwise need to change both the category's boundaries and its buying criteria (564–568). | Prove the problem exists, teach how to evaluate solutions, and prove you are the best vendor. Sell the problem before the solution (572–597). | The product must be obviously new. Expect the "it's just a feature" counterattack (582–586). Needs a strong answer to "why now" (588–591). Costly and slow; small companies need patient investors (607–610). Fast followers often take the leadership spot (614–617). |

Further points:
- Big Fish expansion path: dominate one segment, then move to adjacent segments where your track record carries over. Word of mouth spreads fastest inside tight segments (349–371).
- Her Big Fish case is an enterprise CRM recast as CRM for investment banks (429–539). Eloqua is her Create a New Game case (624–673). Both are single-company stories, not evidence that the method works in general.

**Engine decision rules for small businesses (ClimbOS policy derived from her guidance)**
1. Generate at least three candidate frames: one by abductive reasoning, one adjacent (noting whether it's growing), and one customer-suggested if any exist. Always score the current default frame too.
2. Default style: Big Fish, Small Pond.
3. Allow Head to Head only if (a) the business already leads its local or niche market, or (b) evidence shows a critical mass of buyers understands the category and no clear leader exists, and the owner has resources to move fast.
4. Block Create a New Game unless all of these hold: every existing frame scored as failing to centre the differentiators, a nameable "why now" shift, the product is demonstrably new, and the owner explicitly accepts a long, expensive education plan.
5. Reject a subsegment that can't be listed, has no demonstrable specific need, or is smaller than the near-term sales target requires.

**Comparison pages (ClimbOS application; the book has no comparison-page guidance)**
- Compare against the alternatives best-fit customers actually use (Step 4). That is often a spreadsheet, a manual process or an incumbent, not rival startups.
- Big Fish page against the leader:
  - name the subsegment
  - state the unmet need
  - give the proof of the gap
  - show you are "good enough" on core criteria
  - include a quantified value only if it's evidenced
- Head to Head page: only established criteria, only with hard evidence.
- Never claim a competitor lacks something without evidence.

**Homepage message hierarchy (ClimbOS application)**
1. Category: make it instantly clear what the product is (ch2 406–407).
2. Who it's for: the subsegment, in the language of its best-fit traits.
3. One to four value themes, each with proof (ch8 117–119; ch2 306–310).
4. Optional trend overlay, only if clearly linked to the product (ch11). Her rule is that being a bit boring beats being baffling.
5. Next step.

The sales story order (ch13 30–48) suits a long-form landing page or a demo deck: problem → current approaches and their gaps → "perfect world" criteria → product placed in its category → value themes → call to action.

**Few customers (ch3 67–86 with Step 8)**
- Use exploration mode:
  - one broad, familiar frame
  - deliberately wide exposure across sizes, industries and channels
  - per-segment tracking of how fast people understand, buy, refer and ask for discounts
- Don't tighten until a best-fit pattern appears.
- The book gives no numeric threshold. Any ClimbOS threshold must be labelled as ClimbOS policy.

**Market Frame Selection module (proposed)**
- **Required inputs:**
  - best-fit customer list, or an explicit "none yet" flag
  - grouped alternatives (2–5 groups)
  - attribute ledger with proof
  - value themes (1–4)
  - who-cares traits
  - current default frame
  - near-term sales target
  - owner resources (cash, team, funding)
- **Eligibility:** a best-fit pattern exists for tightened positioning. Otherwise run exploration mode.
- **Steps:**
  1. Baggage scan
  2. Generate candidate frames
  3. For each frame, record the assumptions it triggers: competitors, standard expected features, price band
  4. Check product fit against those assumptions and flag gaps
  5. Choose a style using the gates above
  6. Run the segment size check (sales needed vs segment count)
  7. Trend overlay check
  8. Owner sign-off
- **Outputs:**
  - chosen frame and style, with rejected frames and reasons
  - assumptions accepted vs assumptions to fight
  - the "good enough" basics the product must prove
  - comparison targets
  - message hierarchy
  - price-band implication (ch13 71–79)
  - review triggers: six months, credible competitor entry, regulation, economy, technology shift (ch13 87–153)
- **Success measures** (ClimbOS; the book offers only anecdotes):
  - comprehension accuracy
  - share of leads matching the best-fit traits
  - fewer "how are you better than [wrong competitor]" objections
  - win rate against the main alternative
  - sales cycle length
  - price realisation
- **Stop conditions:**
  - no best-fit pattern, yet the output asks for a narrow frame
  - new-category gates fail
  - subsegment can't be listed or is too small
  - superiority claims lack proof
  - the frame implies standard features the product lacks and the owner won't add or disclose them

### Evaluation case ideas (synthetic)

1. **Pre-pattern bookkeeper**
   - Input: solo bookkeeping practice with 4 clients in 3 unrelated industries. Owner wants to be "the AI-native finance OS".
   - Expected: exploration mode; a broad, familiar frame (bookkeeping); AI shown only as an overlay linked to a concrete capability; a plan for varied outreach and pattern tracking.
   - Hard failure: creating a new category; narrowing to one industry from 4 clients; inventing testimonials.
2. **Clear Big Fish case**
   - Input: scheduling SaaS with 60 customers. 15 ecstatic ones are all physiotherapy chains with 3+ sites. A dominant generic scheduler leads the category. Target is 40 deals this year, and about 2,000 such chains can be listed.
   - Expected: Big Fish frame such as "scheduling for multi-site physio chains"; a comparison page against the leader showing the multi-site need plus good-enough basics; a homepage hierarchy; a segment-size check that passes.
   - Hard failure: Head to Head on general criteria; claiming the leader lacks a feature without evidence; recommending a new category.
3. **Segment too small**
   - Input: same as case 2, but 30 deals are needed and only 100 businesses exist in the segment.
   - Expected: reject the segment or broaden the best-fit definition (ch10 363–365; ch9 75–79).
   - Hard failure: recommending the segment anyway, or inflating its size.
