### Ch 6 Using Leverage (dummy_split_013.html), full read

**Core idea.** Leverage comes from three things together: anticipating what others will predictably do, spotting the pivotal point where a small push releases a lot of stored-up force, and concentrating effort there. Concentration pays because of **threshold effects**: below a certain level of effort, nothing happens (his example is advertising that's too small to register). With limited resources, aim only at targets you can actually move. Dominating a small segment often beats a thin slice of a big market. Organisations, like people, can only work on a few problems at once. A visible full win on a small target builds support better than a tiny gain spread everywhere.

**Decision rules for the engine**
- Anticipation is not prophecy. Use predictable downstream effects of things that have *already happened* (a new competitor opened, a regulation took effect, a platform policy changed, seasonality) and the habits/inertia of others.
- Pivot point = an imbalance: unmet demand, or a skill built in one context that would work in another.
- Concentration test: is the budget above the threshold needed for a detectable effect in one channel/segment? If not, narrow the target, don't spread it.

**Limits.** Rumelt gives no way to *measure* a threshold. Concentration bets are riskier, and his own example admits we can't know if the focused choice did more good.

**Engine application**
- Inputs: monthly budget, owner hours, channel candidates with minimum viable spend/effort estimates (benchmarks marked as estimates), target segments with size and reachability.
- Steps: (1) estimate the minimum effective dose per channel/segment; (2) drop options whose dose exceeds available budget/hours; (3) rank the rest by pivotal fit (imbalance evidence); (4) put ≥70–80% of budget on one primary bet (ClimbOS threshold, not Rumelt's); (5) name one expected leading signal.
- Outputs: one primary bet, why it's pivotal, the dose, what's deliberately unfunded.
- Success: leading signal moves past noise within the window. Stop: dose delivered for the full window with no signal → re-diagnose, don't top up.

**Eval ideas**
- E6-1: $300/month, owner asks for Google Ads + Meta + TikTok + LinkedIn. Expected: says the budget can't clear threshold in four channels and proposes one, with reasoning. Hard fail: splits $75 four ways.
- E6-2: Bakery; new office complex opened nearby last month (fact given). Expected: treats it as an anticipatable downstream effect (weekday lunch/corporate orders) and targets it. Hard fail: generic "post more on Instagram" plan that ignores the given change.

### Ch 10 Focus (dummy_split_017.html), full read

**Core idea.** To work out a firm's real strategy, don't trust its own description or analysts' labels. List the policies that *differ from the industry norm* and ask what single target they all serve. In the can-maker case, the distinctive policies (tech help, rush response, small multi-customer plants, spare lines) all pointed to short production runs. That meant higher cost per unit, but more bargaining power and higher prices. Focus here means a coordinated set of policies serving a chosen slice better than generalists can. It is not just a narrow product line. Focus doesn't guarantee profit; it has to be tied to industry economics, such as who holds bargaining power. Many organisations don't really have a strategy because they spread resources across many goals and never reach a breakthrough anywhere.

**Decision rules**
- Identify the business's current *actual* strategy from what it does differently, not from its tagline. Do this before recommending anything, so ClimbOS doesn't break the thing that's working.
- "Low-cost" is a common wrong guess. Check the cost structure before assuming it.
- Treat a first plausible explanation as provisional. Test it against all the facts (Rumelt shows a student jumping to one).
- Focus should change who has power: fewer captive relationships, more customers per unit of capacity, segments where the firm is hard to replace.

**Engine application**
- Inputs: list of the business's policies (pricing, service levels, hours, minimum order, turnaround, guarantees, customer types), plus competitor norms (scraped/benchmarked, flagged as estimates).
- Steps: (1) diff policies vs. norm; (2) for each distinctive policy ask "which customer needs this?"; (3) find the common target; (4) check economics (price premium, concentration of customers, switching power); (5) write a "current focus" statement and require new tactics to fit it, or flag that they don't.
- Outputs: current-focus statement with evidence, fit/conflict flags on proposed tactics, "strategy unclear" flag when distinctive policies point different ways.
- Success: owner confirms the focus statement; later tactics stay consistent with it. Stop: fewer than 2 distinctive policies → mark "no identifiable focus" and treat building one as the strategic task. Don't invent one.

**Eval ideas**
- E10-1: Print shop with same-day turnaround, no minimum order, higher prices, many small clients. Expected: identifies rush/short-run focus and warns against a bulk-discount campaign aimed at large clients. Hard fail: recommends competing on price.
- E10-2: Business describes itself as "premium quality", but every policy matches the norm. Expected: says no distinctive focus is visible and asks the questions needed. Hard fail: accepts the self-description as the strategy.

### Ch 11 Growth (dummy_split_018.html), full read (block 85 truncated)

**Core idea.** Growth chased for its own sake can destroy value. In the can-maker's acquisition spree, revenue multiplied while shareholder return fell far below the market, because the firm left its real advantage (short runs, flexibility) behind and bought into commodity businesses. Two specific traps:
1. **Riding industry-wide demand growth without an advantage.** The profits get reinvested in capacity and disappear when growth slows.
2. **Growth from substitution** (one product replacing another) has a hard ceiling and stops suddenly once the switch is done.

Acquisitions usually cost a premium and only create value if bought below worth or if the buyer adds unique value. Rumelt lists poor motives: pay rises with size, deal advisers' fees, and the "bigger combined cash flow" logic, which is just adding numbers together. Healthy growth is a *result* of having better products, skills or innovation. It typically shows up as share gains alongside above-average profitability.

**Decision rules**
- Reject any plan whose justification is "grow revenue" without a mechanism tied to an advantage.
- Check unit contribution and capacity: more volume at zero/negative margin, or beyond what can be delivered, isn't growth.
- Ask whether a demand surge is market-wide (everyone benefits, then capacity floods in) or specific to the firm.
- Ask whether growth comes from substitution and where the ceiling is.
- A partnership or contract often beats buying or building ownership (his telecom example: a contract would have done the job without a merger).
- Watch for advisers paid on volume. Flag when a recommendation's source earns on the activity it recommends.

**Limits.** Written about large firms and acquisitions. For SMBs the equivalents are opening a second location, adding product lines, chasing a trending category, or buying ads into a hot niche. The principle carries over, but small firms sometimes need scale to survive, and Rumelt doesn't cover minimum viable scale.

**Engine application**
- Inputs: goal (often a revenue target), current margins/contribution, capacity, source of current advantage (from the Ch 10 step), demand trend data with provenance.
- Steps: (1) restate the revenue goal as "what advantage will this growth come from?"; (2) classify the growth source: share gain from advantage / riding market growth / substitution / buying (location, acquisition) / new extension; (3) for each non-advantage source, run a margin-after-slowdown check and capacity check; (4) prefer extending existing strengths (links to Ch 12).
- Outputs: growth-source classification, risk flags (commodity, substitution ceiling, premium paid), revenue-only plans rejected with reasons.
- Success: growth arrives with stable or improving contribution margin. Stop: margin falls by a set threshold while volume rises → pause scale-up and re-diagnose.

**Eval ideas**
- E11-1: Coffee cart owner wants to open 3 more carts "to double revenue"; current cart margin thin, owner is sole barista. Expected: asks what advantage makes cart #2 profitable, flags labour/capacity, suggests proving unit economics first. Hard fail: projects 4x revenue with no contribution or staffing check.
- E11-2: E-com seller sees a category booming industry-wide (supplied trend) and has no product differentiation. Expected: warns profits may vanish as supply floods in; asks for a differentiator or a time-boxed, capped test. Hard fail: "ride the trend" with scale budget.
- E11-3: Agency proposes merging with another to "combine client bases". Expected: asks what the combination enables that a referral/subcontract deal wouldn't. Hard fail: treats the sum of revenues as the benefit.

### Ch 12 Using Advantage (dummy_split_019.html), full read to 457

**Core idea.** Advantage comes from asymmetries, and it only applies under specific conditions. Press where you're strong and avoid contests that favour others (the start-up with a great material told not to become a clothing brand). An advantage on sale to anyone isn't an advantage. Owning an advantage doesn't make you richer; value grows only when the advantage *grows*. Four routes: deepen it (widen the gap between buyer value and cost), broaden it (apply the underlying skills in new arenas), raise demand for what you're advantaged in, and strengthen what blocks imitation. An advantage is "interesting" only if you can personally do something to raise its value. Two ways improvement stalls: believing pressure alone produces improvement, and weak protection, so rivals free-ride. Extending know-how doesn't use it up, but stretching a brand/reputation can damage the core, so set explicit guardrails. Raising category demand only helps you long-term if you hold most of the scarce supply and rivals respond slowly.

**Engine application**
- Classify each candidate move by which of the four routes it uses. If it uses none, it's probably operational hygiene, not strategy.
- "Build on strengths": extensions should start from the underlying skill, not the product.
- Brand extension guardrails: require 2–3 explicit "won't do" rules before extending (adapted idea).
- Category-demand campaigns (educating a market): only recommend if the business would capture a large share of the new demand (local monopoly, dominant supply, strong brand). Otherwise flag the free-rider risk.
- Avoid "wrestling the gorilla": block plans that need capabilities the business lacks (enterprise SLAs for a 2-person team; national logistics for a local maker) unless there's a capability plan.

**Eval idea**
- E12-1: Only specialty supplier of a rare ingredient in a region, small share of overall demand. Owner wants an "awareness campaign" for the ingredient. Expected: checks capture share. If the business is the dominant supplier, a category campaign is reasonable; if not, rivals benefit. Hard fail: recommends generic education with no capture check.

### Ch 17 Using Your Head (dummy_split_025.html), full read (336, 522 truncated)

**Core idea.** The barrier common to every strategic situation is our own short-sightedness and limited attention. Rumelt uses the old "list the ten most important things, then start on number one" story. The value is in *building* the list: it forces you to find where *important* and *actionable now* overlap, and to turn worries into actions. People faced with messy problems grab the first workable idea (**quick closure**), then spend effort defending it instead of testing it, because dropping a judgment feels uncomfortable. Fast intuition works for some people-reading and pattern-matching, but humans are poor at judging probabilities, their own skill relative to others, and cause and effect. They overweight vivid cases over statistics. Three habits help: tools that direct attention, attacking your own judgment, and writing judgments down beforehand so you can calibrate later.

**Techniques he gives**
- **Kernel as checklist:** diagnosis → guiding policy → coherent action. Turn a local insight into a full argument. He prefers working problem-first, stepping back from "evaluate this action" to "what's the situation".
- **Problem–solution:** given a proposed action, work backwards to which obstacle it addresses. That reveals other solutions to the same obstacle and other ways to frame the problem.
- **Create–destroy:** new options must come from re-reading the facts and must fix weaknesses of existing ones. Fake alternatives don't count: a slight tweak, a straw man, or generic "do nothing / study more".
- **Virtual panel of experts:** critique an idea through the imagined viewpoints of specific, well-known people.
- **Pre-commitment:** write your diagnosis and expected outcome before the discussion or data, then compare.
- Good strategies tend to be **corner solutions**: they commit to one thing rather than a compromise that tries to please everyone.

**Engine application (maps very well to an LLM planner)**
- Inputs: business snapshot, owner's requested tactic (if any), the engine's first-draft diagnosis.
- Steps:
  1. Problem-first: if the owner asks "should I do X?", first produce the diagnosis, and map X backwards to the obstacle it addresses.
  2. Generate ≥3 *structurally different* diagnoses/approaches (different obstacle or different mechanism). Reject alternatives that are tweaks or generic.
  3. Destroy pass: a separate critic prompt attacks each option (critics with distinct personas, e.g. cash-flow CFO, sceptical customer, competitor), recording the strongest objection.
  4. "Important × actionable now" list: top 10 issues, each scored on both; pick #1 from the overlap.
  5. Pre-commit: save the predicted outcome, confidence and review date *before* execution; compare at review (calibration log per business and per policy version).
- Outputs: diagnosis set with destroy-pass objections, chosen corner solution, one #1 action, pre-committed forecast.
- Success: forecasts become calibrated over time (a Brier-style score on pre-committed predictions); owner reports the #1 action was the right focus.
- Stop: if all options fail the destroy pass on a factual objection, gather that fact before planning further. Don't ship the least-bad option silently.

**Limits.** Rumelt gives no method for *how many* alternatives or how long to search. Too much search is its own failure. Set a cap (e.g. 3–5 options, one destroy round).

**Eval ideas**
- E17-1: Owner asks "Should I start a TikTok?" Expected: engine reframes to the obstacle (awareness? trust? conversion?), lists ≥2 other solutions to that obstacle, then answers. Hard fail: a TikTok content calendar with no stated obstacle.
- E17-2: Engine is asked for 3 alternatives. Expected: they differ in diagnosis or mechanism. Hard fail: three versions of the same plan at different budgets, or "do nothing" counted as an option.
- E17-3: Two planning cycles. Expected: cycle 2 cites cycle 1's pre-committed forecast vs actual and adjusts confidence. Hard fail: no record of the prior prediction, or claims it was right without data.

### Ch 18 Keeping Your Head (dummy_split_026.html), full read (392 truncated)

**Core idea.** Good strategy comes from independent assessment, not from following the crowd. Story 1: a transatlantic cable business valued highly by the market, though basic industry analysis showed a commodity product, no proprietary tech, capacity growing faster than demand, and sunk costs. So prices had to collapse, and they did. The market's enthusiasm replaced analysis. Rumelt warns about **closed loops**: if you judge investments by signals that those same investments produce (e.g. measuring "growth" as installed capacity, or following popularity rankings), some questions can't be answered from inside the loop. You need outside data. Also: costs belong to *decisions*, not products. Marginal, average and long-run costs differ, and in a competitive commodity, price falls toward marginal cost.

Story 2: the 2008 crisis. He lists five failure sources: engineering beyond what anyone could analyse; the **smooth-sailing fallacy** (no recent trouble taken as no risk); **risk-seeking incentives** (upside kept, downside passed to others); **social herding**; and the **inside view** (Kahneman–Lovallo), the belief that "our case is different" despite base rates. His examples: most new restaurants fail, yet each founder thinks theirs won't. The fix: look at real data that contradicts the crowd, and at history and other places.

**Decision rules for the engine**
- **Outside view first for every forecast:** start from a base rate for the reference class (similar businesses, channel, offer type, region), then adjust for specific evidence, and show the adjustment. Never accept an owner's or model's single-point "inside" forecast as is.
- **Popularity is not evidence:** "everyone is doing X" or "competitors are all on Y" can be a reason to investigate, never a justification by itself. Ask for the economics.
- **Industry-structure check before entering a hot niche:** commodity offer? copyable? capacity/competitors growing faster than demand? buyers price-sensitive and quick to switch? If most answers are yes, expect margins to compress.
- **Break closed loops:** don't judge success by a metric the plan itself inflates (followers bought by ads, "leads" from incentivised forms, revenue counted before refunds). Require an outside outcome measure.
- **Smooth-sailing check:** a channel that has been stable (one platform, one big client, one ad account) isn't low-risk. Flag concentration and single points of failure.
- **Incentive check:** flag recommendations from sources that profit from the action (affiliate tools, ad platforms' own suggestions).
- **Marginal vs average cost:** when advising on extra orders, discounts or capacity, use the cost of the specific decision.

**Engine application**
- Inputs: forecast request, business type/region, reference-class base rates (with source, date and sample size; mark as "unknown" if none), owner's own estimate.
- Steps: (1) choose reference class; (2) pull the base-rate range; (3) list case-specific evidence, each with a direction and a capped size of adjustment; (4) output forecast as a range anchored on the base rate; (5) if the owner's estimate is far above the base-rate range (e.g. >P90), show it and ask for the evidence.
- Outputs: base-rate-anchored range, adjustment trail, inside-view flag.
- Success: realised outcomes fall inside the stated ranges at about the stated frequency (calibration). Stop: no defensible reference class → say the forecast is unknown and propose a cheap measurement instead of inventing a number.

**Limits.** Base rates for SMB marketing are often poor or from vendors with an interest (itself the incentive problem). The engine must record the source's quality. Rumelt doesn't say how to choose a reference class.

**Eval ideas**
- E18-1: New restaurant owner forecasts profit in month 3 and 40% month-on-month growth. Expected: shows the reference-class reality (high early failure, typical ramp, stated as a range with sources or marked unknown), asks what makes this case different, and gives a range. Hard fail: builds the plan on the owner's figures unchallenged, or makes up a precise failure statistic.
- E18-2: "All my competitors are launching AI chatbots; I need one." Expected: treats as herding, asks what customer problem it solves and what the economics are. Hard fail: recommends it because competitors have it.
- E18-3: Agency gets 85% of revenue from one client and three years of stable growth. Expected: flags concentration risk despite the calm history. Hard fail: calls the revenue "low-risk" because it's been stable.

## 3. Top 5 gap insights for the engine

1. **Outside view for every forecast (Ch 18).** Anchor on reference-class base rates, show adjustments, and flag inside-view optimism. Say "unknown" when no defensible base rate exists.
2. **Reject growth for its own sake (Ch 11).** Every growth goal must name the advantage it comes from. Classify the growth source (advantage / market tide / substitution / bought). Require margin and capacity to hold as volume rises.
3. **Concentrate above threshold (Ch 6, Ch 10).** Small budgets must clear a minimum effective dose in one channel/segment. Spreading below threshold is a hard fail. Work out the business's actual focus from its distinctive policies before adding tactics.
4. **Fight quick closure structurally (Ch 17).** Work problem-first, generate structurally different options, run a destroy pass with critic personas, pick from the "important × actionable now" overlap, and pre-commit forecasts for calibration.
5. **Grow the advantage, not just the business (Ch 12).** Tag moves by route (deepen / broaden / raise demand / protect). Run capture checks before category-education campaigns. Block "gorilla-wrestling" plans that need capabilities the firm lacks.
